# Dam Safety & Surveillance

## Dam Safety Framework

Dam safety is a systematic practice ensuring dams do not fail and cause loss of life, property damage, or environmental harm. The framework encompasses design review, construction quality, ongoing surveillance, emergency preparedness, and regulatory compliance.

### Hazard Classification
Dams are classified by the consequences of failure, not by dam size:
- **High hazard:** Probable loss of life; extensive property/infrastructure damage
- **Significant hazard:** No probable loss of life; significant economic or environmental damage
- **Low hazard:** No expected loss of life; limited economic damage

Hazard classification determines: inspection frequency, IDF criteria, EAP requirements, instrumentation scope, and regulatory oversight level.

## Surveillance Program Components

### Visual Inspection
The most important and cost-effective surveillance tool. Trained inspectors can detect:
- Seepage (new, increasing, or turbid)
- Settlement or displacement (crest, slopes, abutments)
- Cracking (concrete dams — structural, thermal, alkali-aggregate reaction)
- Slope instability (slides, bulges, tension cracks)
- Animal burrows and vegetation (root penetration, tree throw risk)
- Spillway and outlet condition (erosion, blockage, gate operability)
- Downstream erosion and scour

**Inspection types:**
- **Routine / periodic:** Annual to semi-annual by dam owner's engineer
- **Formal (comprehensive):** Every 5 years by independent qualified engineer (FERC: Part 12D inspection)
- **Special:** After significant events (earthquake, flood, unusual observation)
- **Underwater:** For submerged structures (intake, outlet, upstream face)

### Instrumentation Monitoring
Instruments provide quantitative data on parameters that cannot be reliably assessed visually:

**Piezometers / Observation Wells:**
- Measure pore water pressure within dam body, foundation, and abutments
- Types: standpipe (open), vibrating wire, pneumatic
- Critical for: uplift pressure (concrete dams), phreatic surface (embankment dams), seepage paths
- Alarm levels set based on design assumptions and historical trends

**Seepage Measurement:**
- Weirs, flumes, or calibrated collection systems measure seepage flow
- Trend analysis: plot seepage vs. reservoir level over time
- Critical: any increase in seepage not correlated with reservoir rise, or turbid seepage

**Survey Monuments / Deformation Monitoring:**
- Surface monuments (crest, downstream face, abutments)
- Techniques: precise leveling, EDM/total station, GNSS, InSAR, LiDAR
- Concrete dams: plumblines (pendulums), joint meters, extensometers
- Settlement: relevance for embankment dams (differential settlement, crest loss)

**Inclinometers / Shape Arrays:**
- Measure lateral deformation within embankment or foundation
- Detect shear zone development in slopes or foundation

**Temperature:**
- Concrete dams: embedded thermocouples/thermistors for thermal stress monitoring
- Foundation: temperature of seepage water (changes may indicate new seepage paths)

**Strong Motion Accelerographs:**
- Seismic zones: record earthquake shaking at dam for post-earthquake assessment
- Trigger automatic dam inspection protocols when threshold exceeded

## Data Management and Trend Analysis

### Threshold-Based Monitoring
Instruments are assigned threshold levels:
- **Normal (green):** Within expected range for current loading conditions
- **Alert (yellow):** Approaching design assumptions or deviating from trends; increased monitoring frequency
- **Action (red):** Exceeds design assumptions or shows anomalous behavior; immediate investigation and potential EAP activation

### Trend Analysis
- Plot instrument readings vs. time and vs. reservoir level
- Statistical regression: separate effects of reservoir level, temperature (seasonal), and time (irreversible)
- Departures from expected behavior (statistical control charts, HST model: Hydrostatic + Seasonal + Time)
- Compare actual readings to design predictions

### HST (Hydrostatic-Seasonal-Time) Model
For concrete dams, displacements and pressures are modeled as:

Y = f(H) + f(S) + f(T)

where H = hydrostatic (reservoir level) effects, S = seasonal (temperature) effects, T = time-dependent (irreversible) effects. Deviations from the HST model indicate anomalous behavior.

## Emergency Action Plan (EAP)

Required for all high-hazard dams (most jurisdictions):
- **Detection:** How potential failures will be detected (monitoring, inspection)
- **Notification:** Who is contacted and in what order (dam owner → emergency management → downstream population)
- **Inundation mapping:** Predicted flood extent, depth, arrival time for dam breach scenarios
- **Evacuation routes:** Pre-identified routes avoiding inundation zones
- **Exercises:** Annual tabletop exercise; full-scale exercise periodically

## Potential Failure Mode Analysis (PFMA)

Systematic identification and evaluation of ways a dam could fail:
- Internal erosion (piping, backward erosion, suffusion, contact erosion)
- Slope instability (upstream, downstream, foundation)
- Overtopping (inadequate spillway capacity, gate malfunction)
- Foundation failure (sliding, differential movement)
- Structural failure (overstress, deterioration, seismic)
- Operational failure (gate/valve malfunction, power loss, human error)

PFMA is a key input to risk assessment and informs surveillance priorities.

## Dam Safety Regulatory Framework (US)

- **FEMA:** National Dam Safety Program — guidelines, training, grant program
- **FERC:** Regulates non-federal hydropower dams — most rigorous federal oversight
- **USACE:** Federal civil works dams — Dam Safety Program
- **USBR:** Federal reclamation dams — Dam Safety and Technology Development
- **State dam safety programs:** Regulate most non-federal dams (authority varies by state)
- **National Inventory of Dams (NID):** Database of ~92,000 dams in the US
