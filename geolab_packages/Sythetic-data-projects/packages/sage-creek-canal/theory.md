# Sage Creek Main Canal — Reach 7 Rehabilitation: Theory and Technical Background

## Project Description

The Sage Creek Main Canal is a USBR-constructed irrigation delivery system originally built in 1924 as part of the Sage Creek Irrigation Project in southern Idaho. The canal serves approximately 42,000 acres of agricultural land across two counties, delivering water from the Sage Creek Reservoir at a design capacity of 280 cubic feet per second (cfs). The canal extends 12.4 miles from the reservoir headworks to the terminus distribution works, operating from April through October each irrigation season.

Reach 7, spanning from Station 142+00 to Station 166+00 (approximately 2,400 linear feet), was selected as the primary rehabilitation reach following a 2023 condition assessment that identified it as the most severely deteriorated segment of the canal. The concrete lining in Reach 7 was installed in 1962, making it 61 years old at the time of the assessment — exceeding its 40-year design service life by more than 50%. The reach exhibits a combination of structural lining failure, active slope instability along the right bank, and documented seepage losses exceeding target rates by an order of magnitude.

Canal cross-section geometry in Reach 7 is trapezoidal with a bottom width of 12 feet, side slopes of 1.5H:1V (existing), design water depth of 5.5 feet, and a design freeboard of 1.5 feet. The canal operates at a longitudinal slope of approximately 0.0008 ft/ft. The combination of aging infrastructure, problematic subgrade conditions (expansive lacustrine clay and collapsible loess), and a documented right-bank slope failure has rendered this reach a priority for rehabilitation under the USBR WaterSMART Grants for Water and Energy Efficiency program.

The rehabilitation investigation was conducted by HDE Engineering (HDE) in 2023-2025, encompassing a full canal condition assessment, geotechnical investigation with four borings, laboratory testing program, seepage measurement campaign, slope stability analyses, and development of lining design alternatives. This document summarizes the technical findings, engineering theory, and design rationale supporting the recommended rehabilitation approach.

## Regional Geology

Reach 7 of the Sage Creek Main Canal is situated within the eastern margin of the Snake River Plain physiographic province. The site geology reflects a complex depositional history involving aeolian, lacustrine, alluvial, and volcanic processes. Four distinct stratigraphic units were identified in the boring program and are relevant to the canal rehabilitation design.

The uppermost unit, extending from the surface to depths of 0 to 15 feet, consists of wind-deposited loess. This material is classified as ML (silt of low to medium plasticity) to CL (lean clay) per the Unified Soil Classification System. Loess deposits in this region were transported from outwash plains and glaciofluvial sources during Pleistocene glacial periods and redeposited by prevailing southwest winds. The loess exhibits the characteristic open, vertical macropore structure typical of aeolian silts, with apparent cohesion that diminishes sharply upon wetting. Collapse potential ranges from moderate to severe (5.5–10.2% collapse strain at 4 tsf), making this unit particularly hazardous as a canal subgrade.

Beneath the loess, from approximately 5 to 25 feet depth, lies a lacustrine clay deposit of regional extent. This unit is classified as CH (fat clay of high plasticity) and represents sediments deposited in a Pleistocene pluvial lake that occupied the basin prior to regional drainage integration. The clay is dominated by montmorillonite (smectite) clay minerals derived from the weathering of volcanic tuff and ash beds that are widespread across the Snake River Plain. Liquid limits range from 55 to 68, plasticity indices from 30 to 42, and clay fractions from 42 to 58%. This unit is the primary driver of lining heave and subgrade-related panel cracking observed in Reach 7.

Alluvial sand lenses (USCS: SP) were encountered within and below the lacustrine clay at several boring locations. These sands are poorly graded, medium to coarse grained, and exhibit measured hydraulic conductivities of approximately 3.5 × 10⁻³ cm/s — several orders of magnitude more permeable than the surrounding clay. These lenses serve as preferential seepage pathways and are partially responsible for the observed seepage losses. They also complicate slope stability by providing zones of elevated pore water pressure during irrigation season.

Basalt bedrock, associated with late Miocene to Pleistocene Snake River Plain flood basalt volcanism, underlies the section at depths ranging from 15 to 30 feet across Reach 7 (18 to 35 feet from boring data). The basalt surface is irregular due to differential weathering and erosion. Where basalt is close to the surface, it may limit the depth to which subgrade treatment can be performed and must be considered in anchor trench and cutoff design.

## Lining Condition Assessment

A comprehensive condition assessment of 200 concrete lining panels in Reach 7 was completed in September 2023. Panels were evaluated using visual inspection, crack mapping, delamination sounding, and selective concrete coring. Four condition categories were assigned.

Good condition panels (14% of total, approximately 28 panels) exhibited no significant cracking, uniform surface texture, no delamination, and structurally sound concrete. These panels had measured compressive strengths of 3,200–3,850 psi and effective thicknesses of 3.0–3.5 inches, within or near design specifications.

Fair condition panels (26% of total, approximately 52 panels) exhibited minor to moderate cracking (less than 1/8-inch width), localized surface scaling, and minor delamination near panel edges. Compressive strengths ranged from 2,400–3,200 psi with effective thicknesses of 2.5–3.0 inches. These panels retain some structural integrity but are expected to transition to poor condition within 5–10 years without intervention.

Poor condition panels (39% of total, approximately 78 panels) exhibited extensive cracking (crack widths 1/8–1/2 inch), surface spalling, significant delamination, and visible joint seal failure. Several poor panels showed displacement consistent with subgrade heave. Compressive strengths ranged from 1,400–2,400 psi. Effective thicknesses were measured at 2.0–2.5 inches, reflecting surface loss from freeze-thaw scaling and carbonation.

Failed panels (21% of total, approximately 42 panels) were displaced, broken into multiple fragments, or exhibited total loss of structural integrity. Many failed panels showed upward displacement of 2 to 6 inches relative to adjacent panels, clearly attributable to expansive clay swell beneath the lining. Compressive strengths in intact fragments ranged from 980 to 1,400 psi, well below the 3,000 psi design strength. Effective thicknesses in failed zones were as low as 2.0 inches.

Alkali-silica reaction (ASR) was identified as a significant degradation mechanism in approximately 30% of lining panels. The original 1962 concrete used locally sourced volcanic aggregate (rhyolite and basalt) that contains reactive silica phases (cristobalite, tridymite, volcanic glass). These react with the highly alkaline pore solution in cement paste to form a hygroscopic gel that expands upon moisture absorption, generating internal stresses that cause characteristic map cracking (crazing), surface pop-outs, and gel exudate. ASR severity ratings ranged from Level 1 (minor gel deposits without cracking) to Level 3 (extensive map cracking with gel exudate and measurable expansion), with Level 2-3 panels concentrated in sections with the highest moisture exposure.

Freeze-thaw damage is pervasive throughout the reach, manifesting as surface scaling, delamination of the top 1/4–1/2 inch of concrete, and popouts at aggregate locations. Idaho climatic data indicates 80–120 freeze-thaw cycles per year at this site, well above the threshold for significant concrete deterioration in non-air-entrained concrete. The original 1962 concrete was not air-entrained, consistent with practice of that era. Air void analysis of cores confirmed the absence of an adequate air void system (spacing factor > 0.010 inch) in all tested specimens.

The overall effective lining thickness across Reach 7, weighted by panel condition, ranges from 2.5 to 3.0 inches against a nominal design thickness of 3.5 inches — representing a 14–29% reduction in cross-section available to resist subgrade heave loads and hydraulic pressures.

## Right Bank Slope Failure

Active slope instability along the right bank of Reach 7 was documented during the 2023 condition assessment and confirmed by geotechnical investigation in early 2025. The failure zone extends approximately 180 linear feet along the reach, centered near Station 152+40. Field observations recorded a tension crack scarp of 2–3 feet height at the top of the bank, 18 inches of lateral bulge measured at mid-slope, and localized surface sloughing along the upper bank face.

The failure mechanism is a translational slide along a thin, slickensided clay band at the contact between the loess and lacustrine clay units, at depths of approximately 8–12 feet below the canal bank top. This contact zone exhibits residual shear strength parameters (c'r = 40–80 psf, φ'r = 12–14°) substantially lower than the peak strength of the overlying loess (c' = 200–400 psf, φ' = 28–32°). The slickenside surfaces reflect repeated prior displacement that has reduced the clay minerals to their residual (fully softened) state.

During the irrigation season, canal seepage saturates the loess and raises pore water pressures along the failure surface. The loess transitions from a dry, apparently cohesive material (apparent cohesion 150–200 psf from capillary tension) to a saturated, near-zero effective cohesion material upon wetting. This wetting-induced strength loss, combined with elevated pore pressures at the clay contact, results in a factor of safety against sliding of only 0.92 using residual strength parameters — indicating that active failure is occurring under current irrigation conditions.

Spencer's method of slices was used for slope stability analysis, accounting for both inter-slice normal and shear forces and satisfying both force and moment equilibrium. Analyses were conducted for four phreatic surface conditions: dry (no irrigation), beginning-of-season, mid-season (steady seepage), and end-of-season (drawdown). The critical mid-season condition with peak strength parameters yields FS = 1.15, marginally stable. With residual strength parameters representing the existing slickensided failure surface, FS = 0.92, confirming active failure.

The proposed remediation involves regrading the right bank from the existing approximately 1.5H:1V slope to a flattened 3H:1V configuration, installation of a drainage blanket behind the regraded bank, and installation of a French drain system at the toe of the bank to intercept and divert seepage. Stability analyses for the remediated condition yield FS = 1.35 (residual strength, with drainage) to 1.52 (peak strength, with drainage), satisfying the project target of FS ≥ 1.30 for the critical sustained-loading condition.

## Seepage Loss Quantification

Seepage losses from Reach 7 were quantified using two methods: inflow-outflow measurements in 2023 and a ponding test conducted on a 600-foot isolated test section in April 2025.

Inflow-outflow measurements were taken at five dates during the 2023 irrigation season using calibrated staff gauges and velocity measurements at the upstream and downstream ends of Reach 7. Measured losses ranged from 7.1 to 8.2 cfs over the 2,400-foot reach, with an average of 7.7 cfs per 1,000 linear feet. At typical full-supply delivery of 220 cfs through the reach, this represents a 3.5% loss rate — but expressed relative to the USBR target seepage rate of 0.5–1.0 cfs/1,000 ft for a concrete-lined canal in good condition, the measured losses are 8 to 15 times above acceptable levels.

The annualized seepage loss is estimated at approximately 5,400 acre-feet per year (AF/yr) from Reach 7 alone, representing water that is unavailable for delivery to irrigators. At a water value of $50 per acre-foot (reflecting the blended agricultural value of water rights in the district), the economic loss from Reach 7 seepage is approximately $270,000 per year. This figure does not include indirect costs from waterlogging of adjacent agricultural land, increased drainage pumping requirements, or the contribution of seepage to the slope instability documented above.

The ponding test was conducted by isolating a 600-foot section of the failed lining, filling it to the normal operating water depth of 5.5 feet, and monitoring water level decline over a 72-hour period with the inflow valve closed. The test recorded an average seepage rate of 28–35 gallons per minute over the monitoring period (24–35 gpm), consistent with the inflow-outflow measurements. The seepage rate stabilized at approximately 28 gpm after 48 hours, suggesting that the surrounding loess had reached near-saturation and a quasi-steady seepage condition had been achieved.

SEEP/W finite element seepage modeling calibrated to these field measurements indicates that the majority of seepage (approximately 65%) occurs through failed and poor-condition panels where the concrete is cracked or displaced, with the remainder occurring through open expansion joints where the original PVC waterstop has deteriorated. The effective hydraulic conductivity of the existing lining, back-calculated from the ponding test data, is approximately 5 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s — comparable to moderately fractured rock and approximately 1,000 times more permeable than intact concrete.

## Subsurface Conditions

The subsurface model for Reach 7 was developed from four geotechnical borings (BH-SC-01 through BH-SC-04) drilled in January 2025, supplemented by soil test pits, hand auger borings, and review of prior USBR investigation records. A four-unit stratigraphic model was established, with the following engineering properties summarized from laboratory testing.

Unit 1 — Loess (ML/CL): Encountered from ground surface to depths of 6–14 feet. Dry unit weight γd = 82–90 pcf, natural moisture content w = 8–14%, specific gravity Gs = 2.68. Atterberg limits: LL = 28–38, PL = 18–24, PI = 10–16. The loess is lightly overconsolidated under dry conditions but exhibits moderate to severe collapse potential (CP = 5.5–10.2%) when inundated under load. Hydraulic conductivity k = 1.2–3.8 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s (vertical), anisotropy ratio kh/kv estimated at 2–5 based on macropore structure.

Unit 2 — Lacustrine Clay (CH): Encountered from 5–14 feet to 18–26 feet depth. Dry unit weight γd = 72–82 pcf, natural moisture content w = 22–32%, specific gravity Gs = 2.74. Atterberg limits: LL = 55–68, PL = 22–28, PI = 30–42. Clay fraction 42–58%, activity A = 0.70–0.75, confirming montmorillonite as the dominant clay mineral. Swell pressure at in-situ void ratio ranges from 3,600 to 6,500 psf. Free swell ranges from 4.5 to 8.1%. Hydraulic conductivity k = 1.5–4.2 × 10⁻⁷ cm/s (vertical). Preconsolidation pressure σ'p estimated at 1.2–1.8 tsf from oedometer tests.

Unit 3 — Alluvial Sand (SP): Encountered as discontinuous lenses at depths of 12–22 feet, typically 2–5 feet thick. Dry unit weight γd = 98–108 pcf, natural moisture content w = 8–15%, D10 = 0.18–0.32 mm, D60 = 0.55–0.95 mm, Cu = 1.8–3.2, Cc = 0.9–1.2. Hydraulic conductivity k = 2.8–4.2 × 10⁻³ cm/s from slug tests.

Unit 4 — Basalt Bedrock: Encountered at depths of 18–35 feet (elevation 3,640–3,658 ft). Surface is weathered and irregular. RQD values of 45–72% in the upper weathered zone, increasing to 82–90% in fresher basalt below. Hydraulic conductivity from packer tests k = 1.2–8.5 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s (fracture-controlled).

Groundwater was encountered in BH-SC-02 and BH-SC-03 at depths of 14.2 and 16.8 feet respectively during drilling in January (non-irrigation season). During the irrigation season, groundwater elevations are expected to rise by 3–6 feet based on historical water table data from nearby monitoring wells.

## Loess Properties

Loess is a wind-deposited (aeolian) silt that accumulates in sheets across periglacial landscapes downwind of glacial outwash plains. The loess at Reach 7 is composed of quartz and feldspar silt-sized particles (0.002–0.06 mm) in a metastable open structure stabilized by clay bridges and carbonite cement at particle contacts. This structure gives dry loess an apparent cohesion of 150–200 psf from matric suction and a dry unit weight of 82–90 pcf — considerably lighter than the theoretical minimum void ratio for the particle gradation.

The defining geotechnical hazard of loess is hydrocompression (collapse), the sudden, irreversible decrease in volume that occurs when loess is wetted under load. The open macropore structure, which gives the deposit its characteristic vertical cliffs and apparent strength when dry, disintegrates upon wetting as capillary menisci are destroyed and clay bridges dissolve. The result is a dramatic reduction in void ratio at constant effective stress — potentially 5–10% volumetric strain occurring over minutes to hours once water reaches the collapsible horizon.

Collapse potential (CP) is quantified by the single-oedometer method (ASTM D5333): a sample at natural moisture content is loaded to the overburden plus applied stress (typically 4 tsf for canal subgrades), then inundated. The collapse strain is:

CP = Δe / (1 + e₀) × 100%

where Δe is the change in void ratio upon inundation and e₀ is the void ratio at the initial moisture content. The Jennings and Knight classification rates CP of 1–5% as moderate, 5–10% as moderately severe, and greater than 10% as severe. The five samples tested from Reach 7 yielded CP values of 5.5, 6.2, 7.1, 8.8, and 10.2%, classifying the loess as moderate to severe collapse potential — a serious hazard for any canal subgrade that will be repeatedly wetted during irrigation operations.

The combination of collapse potential and proximity to the canal bottom means that differential settlement of the loess subgrade during early canal operation has likely contributed to the observed cracking and joint offset in many of the existing panels, independent of the expansive clay effects. However, the loess below the existing canal has likely already undergone significant wetting-induced collapse since 1962, and collapse potential of material immediately beneath the canal may be lower than that measured in adjacent unirrigated areas.

The transition from collapsible loess behavior to softened, near-zero cohesion behavior under saturated conditions is also critical to the slope stability problem described above. Apparent cohesion from suction (150–200 psf) disappears rapidly upon wetting, and the residual effective cohesion of the loess in the saturated state is near zero (c' < 50 psf). This transition from dry to saturated strength is one of the most dramatic in geotechnical engineering and is the primary reason that canal bank failures in loess are rapid and difficult to arrest once initiated.

## Expansive Clay Assessment

The lacustrine clay of Unit 2 is the most significant geotechnical hazard in Reach 7 from a lining design perspective. The clay is dominated by montmorillonite (smectite), a 2:1 sheet silicate clay mineral with an extremely high specific surface area (up to 800 m²/g) and a large isomorphous substitution deficit that creates a strong negative surface charge. This charge is satisfied by hydrated exchangeable cations in the interlayer space, and the clay swells as water molecules are attracted to and intercalated between the silicate sheets in a process known as double-layer expansion.

The activity of the Reach 7 lacustrine clay (A = PI / clay fraction) ranges from 0.70 to 0.75. Activity above 0.75 is the threshold typically associated with dominant montmorillonite, but the values here, combined with the high plasticity indices (PI = 30–42) and direct X-ray diffraction analysis confirming montmorillonite as the primary mineral, leave no doubt as to the expansive nature of the clay. Companion soil swelling classifications place these soils in the "very high" expansion potential category (PI > 35, swell > 3%).

Swell pressure was measured using the ASTM D4546 constant volume method on seven undisturbed tube samples. The constant volume method holds the sample at its natural void ratio and measures the stress required to prevent swelling — this is the most directly applicable method for evaluating swell pressure acting on a rigid lining panel. Results ranged from 3,600 to 6,500 psf across the seven samples. Free swell (measured by inundating the sample under a token 144 psf surcharge) ranged from 4.5 to 8.1%.

The significance of these values for lining design is best illustrated by comparing the swell uplift force against the panel self-weight. A standard 12-foot wide × 10-foot long × 3.5-inch thick concrete panel weighs approximately 50 psf. The swell pressure of 3,600–6,500 psf exceeds the panel self-weight by a factor of 72–130. The concrete panel provides essentially no resistance to clay swell — the clay will move the panel as though it were weightless. Anchoring the panel to resist swell is not feasible; the swell energy is simply too large. The only effective design approach is to either prevent moisture changes from reaching the clay (via a low-permeability liner) or chemically treat the clay to eliminate its swell potential (via lime stabilization).

## Slope Stability Analysis

Slope stability analyses were performed using Spencer's method of slices implemented in SLOPE/W (GeoStudio). Spencer's method satisfies both force and moment equilibrium and accounts for inter-slice forces, making it appropriate for the complex geometry and multiple failure surfaces considered in this analysis. All analyses were performed in terms of effective stress with pore water pressures defined by imported phreatic surfaces from the SEEP/W model.

Five loading conditions were analyzed:

1. Existing geometry, dry conditions (no irrigation), peak strength: FS = 1.68. The existing 1.5H:1V slope is nominally stable under dry conditions with peak strength parameters. This explains why the bank was not obviously unstable during pre-1962 dry-land conditions.

2. Existing geometry, mid-season phreatic surface, peak strength: FS = 1.15. Elevated pore pressures from canal seepage reduce the factor of safety to marginally acceptable levels. Small disturbances or variations in clay strength could trigger failure.

3. Existing geometry, mid-season phreatic surface, residual strength at clay contact: FS = 0.92. This is the controlling condition for the existing failure. With the slickensided failure surface fully developed and pore pressures at mid-season levels, the slope is actively failing.

4. Remediated geometry (3H:1V), mid-season phreatic surface with drainage blanket, peak strength: FS = 1.52. The combination of flatter slope and active drainage provides an adequate safety margin against first-time failure through the regraded loess.

5. Remediated geometry (3H:1V), mid-season phreatic surface with drainage blanket, residual strength at clay contact: FS = 1.35. Even with residual strength along the clay contact (which will remain in place despite regrading), the remediated slope with drainage satisfies the FS ≥ 1.30 target for sustained loading.

Residual shear strength parameters were established from consolidated-drained (CD) direct shear tests conducted in a 4-inch square shear box at three displacement rates, with the sample sheared to large displacement in both directions to achieve a fully remolded surface. Residual parameters for the lacustrine clay are c'r = 40–80 psf (from multiple tests, scatter likely due to variability in mineral composition) and φ'r = 12–14°.

Peak strength parameters for the loess are c' = 200–400 psf and φ' = 28–32° (from CD triaxial tests at three confining stresses). The peak cohesion includes both true cohesion from cementation and apparent cohesion from matric suction; the design analyses conservatively used c' = 200 psf for saturated conditions.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the factor of safety for the remediated slope is most sensitive to the friction angle of the clay contact surface (ΔFSI per degree = 0.04) and to the depth of the phreatic surface below the slope surface (ΔFSI per foot of drawdown = 0.03). The drainage blanket design targets a phreatic surface at least 3 feet below the slope surface throughout the irrigation season, providing a 0.09 improvement in FS relative to undrained conditions.

## Subgrade Treatment Design

Two primary subgrade treatment strategies are available to address the expansive clay and collapsible loess beneath the canal lining: lime stabilization and geomembrane seepage control.

Lime stabilization uses calcium oxide (CaO, quicklime) or calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂, hydrated lime) to modify the clay mineral structure through ion exchange, flocculation, and pozzolanic reaction. When lime is mixed with expansive clay, calcium ions from the lime displace the exchangeable cations on the montmorillonite surface, reducing the diffuse double layer thickness and dramatically decreasing swell potential. The pozzolanic reaction (lime + clay minerals + water → calcium silicate hydrates + calcium aluminate hydrates) provides long-term cementation that further reduces plasticity and swell potential.

The Eades and Grim pH test (ASTM D6276) was used to establish the minimum lime content for full soil modification: soil-lime mixtures at varying lime contents are mixed with distilled water and the pH is measured after one hour. The minimum lime content required to achieve and maintain pH ≥ 12.4 indicates the amount needed to satisfy the soil's lime demand. Testing indicated that 6% CaO by dry weight satisfies this criterion for the Reach 7 lacustrine clay.

At 6% CaO treatment to an 8-inch depth (one lift of compacted subgrade below the new lining), the expected reduction in Plasticity Index is from PI = 30–42 to PI = 8–15, and the expected elimination of measurable swell potential (swell pressure < 500 psf, free swell < 0.5%). These values are based on correlations from the literature (Little 1995, Transportation Research Record) and confirmed by trial mix testing performed during the geotechnical investigation. The treated soil is compacted to 95% of modified Proctor maximum dry density (ASTM D1557) at or near optimum moisture content (OMC + 0 to +2%).

The 40-mil HDPE geomembrane provides a near-impermeable barrier (hydraulic conductivity approximately 1 × 10⁻¹² cm/s for intact material) that prevents moisture from reaching the subgrade, thereby preventing both collapse of the loess and swell of the clay. The geomembrane reduces seepage through the lining system by 99.7% relative to a new concrete-only lining. However, the geomembrane alone does not address the existing expansive clay that is already partially swelled under the existing lining — some moisture will have already entered the subgrade through failed panels, and the clay may be at a partially elevated moisture content at the start of construction.

The recommended combined system uses lime stabilization to chemically alter the clay and eliminate its swell potential, followed by a sand cushion layer (4 inches of clean sand) to protect the geomembrane from the lime-treated subgrade, then the 40-mil HDPE geomembrane, and finally the new 4-inch reinforced concrete lining. This combination addresses the expansive clay root cause (lime treatment), provides a redundant seepage barrier (geomembrane), and protects the new concrete from frost and subgrade movement (sand cushion).

## Seepage Modeling

Steady-state seepage through the canal lining and underlying subgrade was modeled using SEEP/W (GeoStudio) with a finite element mesh refined at the lining-subgrade interface. The model geometry is a cross-section perpendicular to the canal axis, extending 50 feet from the canal centerline on each side and 30 feet below the canal invert. Hydraulic boundary conditions are: total head equal to the full supply level (5.5-foot depth) along the wetted perimeter, free-draining boundary at the model base.

Material hydraulic conductivities used in the calibrated model are as follows. Existing cracked concrete lining: k = 5 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s (back-calculated from ponding test). Loess (Unit 1): k = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ cm/s (geometric mean of laboratory measurements). Lacustrine clay (Unit 2): k = 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm/s (geometric mean of laboratory measurements). Alluvial sand (Unit 3): k = 3.5 × 10⁻³ cm/s (geometric mean of slug test results). New intact concrete lining: k = 1 × 10⁻⁸ cm/s (typical value for good quality concrete). HDPE geomembrane (intact): k = 1 × 10⁻¹² cm/s (manufacturer specification). HDPE geomembrane (with defects at 1/acre installation quality): k_effective = 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ cm/s.

Modeled seepage rates per unit length of canal for the four scenarios are:

- Existing condition (cracked concrete): 15.8 cfs/1,000 ft (model) vs. 7.7 cfs/1,000 ft (measured). The factor-of-two discrepancy reflects the model's assumption of uniform cracking; the higher measured value in some sections is offset by zones of better lining in others.
- New PCC lining only (no geomembrane): 0.8 cfs/1,000 ft. A 95% reduction from existing, but still above the 0.5 cfs/1,000 ft target for the design life.
- Geomembrane + new PCC lining: 0.05 cfs/1,000 ft. A 99.7% reduction from existing and well below the target.
- Lime treatment + geomembrane + new PCC: 0.05 cfs/1,000 ft. The lime treatment does not materially affect the seepage rate (clay is already low-permeability) but addresses the swell mechanism.

The five-order-of-magnitude permeability contrast between the alluvial sand (k = 3.5 × 10⁻³ cm/s) and the lacustrine clay (k = 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm/s) creates strong lateral flow concentration in the sand lenses during the current seeping condition. Seepage vectors in the model show nearly horizontal flow in the sand lenses, discharging at the slope face and contributing directly to the slope stability problem. The geomembrane interrupts this seepage pathway at the source.

## Lining Design

The recommended lining system — Alternative A — consists of the following components from subgrade upward:

1. Lime-stabilized subgrade: 6% CaO by dry weight mixed to an 8-inch depth using a rotary tiller or reclaimer, conditioned for 48 hours (mellowing), remixed, then compacted to 95% of modified Proctor MDD (ASTM D1557) at OMC + 0 to +2%. A 7-day cure period (minimum) must be achieved before placing subsequent layers. The cured surface must be protected from traffic by a geotextile and from rain by sheeting.

2. Sand cushion: 4 inches of clean, well-graded sand (SW or SP-SM, less than 5% fines, D10 > 0.15 mm) compacted to 95% of standard Proctor. The cushion protects the geomembrane from puncture by lime-treated aggregate and distributes any differential subgrade movement to avoid stress concentrations in the membrane.

3. 40-mil HDPE geomembrane: Smooth HDPE conforming to GRI GM-13 properties. Panels deployed with a minimum 12-inch overlap at transverse seams and 6-inch overlap at longitudinal seams, all field welded using extrusion or fusion welding equipment. 100% of seams must be tested by air lance (extrusion welds) or air pressure (dual-track fusion welds) before placement of concrete. The membrane is anchored at the canal top of bank in a continuous anchor trench.

4. 4-inch reinforced Portland cement concrete lining: Concrete mix design requirements: f'c = 4,000 psi at 28 days, w/c ≤ 0.45, 6 ± 1% air entrainment (ASTM C260 admixture), minimum Type II portland cement with supplemental SCM (30% Class F fly ash or 40% slag cement by mass of cementite). Non-reactive aggregate required per ASTM C1260 (mortar bar expansion < 0.10% at 14 days). Reinforcement: 6×6-W4.0×W4.0 welded wire fabric (WWF), placed at mid-depth of slab. Panel joints at 12-foot spacing in both directions. PVC waterstop (6-inch flat profile) installed at all joints, embedded in both adjacent panels. Formed construction joints at panel ends with a 3/4-inch compressible foam backer and polyurethane sealant.

The fly ash or slag cement SCM serves a dual purpose: reducing the alkali content of the cement paste (lowering the risk of new ASR in any marginal aggregate) and improving long-term durability through densification of the paste microstructure. The 30% Class F fly ash dosage reduces equivalent alkali content by approximately 25%, supplementing the benefit of using non-reactive aggregate.

Canal capacity for the rehabilitated Reach 7 is calculated using Manning's equation with n = 0.013 for new smooth concrete: Q = (1.49/0.013) × A × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2). For the design cross-section (bottom width 12 ft, side slopes 1.5H:1V, depth 5.5 ft, S = 0.0008 ft/ft): A = 111.4 ft², R = 3.12 ft, Q = 295 cfs. This exceeds the design capacity of 280 cfs, confirming that the proposed geometry and lining roughness are adequate.

## Construction Sequence

The construction sequence for Reach 7 rehabilitation is organized into six phases designed to maintain partial canal operations (diversion to alternate reaches) while minimizing the active construction duration.

Phase 1 — Demolition and Excavation (Weeks 1–3): Remove existing concrete lining panels by sawcutting joints and lifting with excavator. Haul broken concrete to on-site staging for disposal or use as aggregate fill in non-structural applications (with owner approval). Excavate the existing subgrade within the lining footprint to the depth required for subgrade treatment (8 inches of lime treatment plus 4 inches of sand cushion equals approximately 12 inches total, plus adjustment for existing grade). Excavate the right bank failure zone and regrade to 3H:1V in the failure section; install drainage blanket (12 inches of clean gravel, minimum k = 10⁻² cm/s) and perforated collector pipe (4-inch HDPE, Schedule 40, perforated, geotextile wrapped) at the base of the new bank.

Phase 2 — Lime Stabilization (Weeks 4–5): Scarify existing subgrade to 8-inch depth using rotary tiller or reclaimer. Apply pulverized quicklime (CaO, 93% purity minimum) at the design rate (6% by dry weight of soil, approximately 23 lb/yd² for the 8-inch treatment depth at γd = 88 pcf). Mix thoroughly using multiple tilling passes until lime is uniformly distributed and no CaO lumps greater than 1/2 inch are visible. Allow 48-hour mellowing period with moisture maintained above OMC by light watering if needed. Remix and compact to 95% modified Proctor in 4-inch lifts. Allow minimum 7-day cure before placing sand cushion; test cured surface with DCP (dynamic cone penetrometer) to confirm adequate strength (CBR ≥ 10).

Phase 3 — Geomembrane Installation (Weeks 6–7): Place and compact 4-inch sand cushion. Survey and verify subgrade elevations. Deploy HDPE geomembrane panels from rolls. Weld all seams; test 100% of seam welds (dual-track seams at 30 psi for 5 minutes, extrusion welds by air lance); repair any failed locations. Install anchor trench at top of bank (minimum 12 inches wide × 12 inches deep, concrete backfilled). Mark panel edges at joint locations for PVC waterstop placement.

Phase 4 — Concrete Placement (Weeks 8–11): Place concrete using slip-form paving machine (canal paving) or hand-formed panels as appropriate to site access. Place 6×6-W4.0×W4.0 WWF at mid-depth using chairs; maintain minimum 3/4-inch bottom cover over geomembrane. Install PVC waterstop at all joint locations prior to concrete placement in adjacent panel. Cure concrete with white-pigmented curing compound (ASTM C309 Type 1-D) applied immediately after finishing. Allow minimum 7-day cure before sealing joints.

Phase 5 — Joint Sealing (Week 12): Clean all panel joints with high-pressure water blast and wire brush. Install 3/4-inch diameter closed-cell backer rod. Apply two-part polyurethane sealant (Shore A hardness 25–35, bond-to-concrete strength ≥ 40 psi, compatible with HDPE geomembrane). Seal all perimeter anchor trench joints.

Phase 6 — Commissioning (Week 13): Fill canal at a controlled rate not to exceed 6 inches per hour to allow air to escape from the geomembrane-concrete interface and prevent hydraulic uplift on the new lining. Inspect for distress at the 2-foot, 4-foot, and full-supply (5.5-foot) water levels. Document any anomalies. Conduct initial seepage measurement (inflow-outflow) within the first two weeks of operation. Compare against baseline measurements to verify seepage reduction performance (target: ≤ 0.5 cfs/1,000 ft).

## Cost-Benefit Analysis

The total estimated project cost for Reach 7 rehabilitation under Alternative A (lime-stabilized subgrade + sand cushion + 40-mil HDPE geomembrane + 4-inch reinforced PCC) is $1,020,000. This includes: mobilization and site work ($85,000), right bank stabilization including regrading and drainage ($95,000), demolition of existing lining ($75,000), lime stabilization ($110,000), sand cushion ($35,000), HDPE geomembrane ($145,000), concrete lining ($385,000), joint sealing ($45,000), and engineering and construction management (included in unit costs via 15% overhead). Contingency is included at 15% of construction subtotal.

The primary quantifiable benefit is the annual water savings from seepage reduction. The 99.7% seepage reduction from the combined geomembrane + PCC system converts the existing 5,400 AF/yr loss to approximately 16 AF/yr — a savings of 5,384 AF/yr. At $50/AF, this represents $269,200 per year in recovered water value. Rounding to the $270,000/yr figure used for project economics.

Simple payback period T = C_project / S_annual = $1,020,000 / $270,000 = 3.78 years, approximately 3.8 years. This is an exceptionally favorable payback for infrastructure rehabilitation.

The 40-year net present value (NPV) of the project, calculated at a discount rate of 3.5% (consistent with USBR project evaluation criteria) and assuming a 40-year lining service life with zero salvage value, is $1,180,000. This represents the discounted value of avoided seepage losses over the project life minus the project cost. Alternative B (PCC lining only, no geomembrane or lime treatment) yields a 40-year NPV of $380,000 due to higher residual seepage losses. Alternative C (shotcrete overlay) yields NPV of $-120,000 due to high unit cost and limited swell resistance. Alternative D (geomembrane only, no new concrete) yields NPV of $820,000 due to lower initial cost but reduced service life.

The project is eligible for WaterSMART Grants for Water and Energy Efficiency (Title XVI), which can fund up to 50% of total project cost for qualifying water conservation measures. With 50% federal cost-share, the irrigation district's net investment would be $510,000 and the simple payback would fall to 1.9 years. The irrigation district has submitted a Letter of Intent for FY 2026 WaterSMART funding with HDE's support.

Secondary benefits not captured in the NPV analysis include: elimination of the active slope failure and associated liability, reduction in waterlogging of adjacent farmland (estimated 120 acres currently affected), reduction in drainage pumping energy costs (estimated $18,000/yr), and improved water delivery reliability at the downstream end of Reach 7 where farmers have reported chronic short delivery during peak demand periods due to transmission losses.
