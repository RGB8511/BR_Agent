# Rock Coring Methods & Core Logging

## Core Barrel Systems

### Wireline Coring
The standard system for geotechnical rock coring. The core barrel (inner tube + outer barrel + diamond bit) remains at the bottom of the hole while the inner tube is retrieved to the surface through the drill string using an overshot assembly on a wireline. Eliminates the need to trip the entire drill string to recover core.

**Wireline sizes (DCDMA standard):**
- **AQ:** 27.0 mm core diameter. Rarely used — too small for meaningful testing.
- **BQ:** 36.5 mm core. Small; used for deep mineral exploration.
- **NQ/NQ3:** 47.6 mm core (NQ2: 50.5 mm). Most common for geotechnical investigation. Good balance of core size, borehole diameter, and rod handling.
- **HQ/HQ3:** 61.1 mm core (HQ2: 63.5 mm). Preferred for dam foundations, tunnels, and projects requiring laboratory testing. Better core recovery in fractured rock.
- **PQ/PQ3:** 85.0 mm core. Large diameter for high-value projects, very fractured rock, or when large test specimens are required.

### Non-Wireline (Conventional) Coring
Entire drill string tripped to recover core. Slower but uses simpler, less expensive equipment. Common in shallow holes or where wireline equipment is not available. Sizes: N (54.7 mm core), H (76.2 mm core).

### Triple-Tube Core Barrels
Inner tube contains a split inner liner that holds the core. When extracted, the liner is split open to expose the core without disturbing it. Critical for soft rock, highly fractured rock, or core that would be damaged by pushing it out of a standard inner tube. Adds cost but dramatically improves recovery in difficult rock.

### Integral Sampling
Developed by Rocha (1971). A reinforcing bar is grouted into a pilot hole, then over-cored. The grout bonds fragments together, preserving the exact in-situ position and orientation of every piece. Used for critical discontinuity studies in dam foundations. Expensive and slow but provides the highest quality structural data.

## Core Orientation

### Purpose
Determining the true dip and dip direction of discontinuities intersected by the core. Without orientation, only the apparent angle between the discontinuity and the core axis is known — not its real spatial position.

### Methods
- **Bottom-of-hole impression (Craelius, EZY-Mark, ACT):** A soft clay or wax impression device lowered to the bottom of the hole before each core run captures the orientation of the stub left from the previous run. The impression is oriented using gravity (plumb bob) or magnetic compass in the tool.
- **Core orientation systems (Reflex ACT, Ballmark):** Mechanical or electronic systems that mark the bottom-of-hole orientation on the core as it enters the barrel.
- **Downhole televiewer (acoustic/optical):** Images the borehole wall after coring. Discontinuities visible in the image have known orientation from the tool's internal compass and inclinometer. Can provide orientation data without physically orienting the core.

### Alpha and Beta Angles
- **Alpha (α):** Angle between the discontinuity plane and the core axis. Measured directly from unoriented core.
- **Beta (β):** Rotation angle around the core axis from the orientation reference line to the downhole direction of maximum dip of the discontinuity. Requires oriented core.

With α, β, borehole inclination, and borehole azimuth, the true dip and dip direction of the discontinuity are calculated using stereographic projection or analytical formulas.

## Core Recovery Indices

### Total Core Recovery (TCR)
TCR = (Total length of core recovered / Total length of core run) × 100%

All pieces counted regardless of condition. Indicates overall core barrel performance and rock quality.

### Solid Core Recovery (SCR)
SCR = (Length of core in pieces > 100 mm / Total length of core run) × 100%

Also called "mechanical break-free recovery." More indicative of rock mass quality than TCR because it excludes small fragments that may result from drilling damage.

### Rock Quality Designation (RQD)
RQD = (Sum of lengths of intact core pieces ≥ 100 mm / Total length of core run) × 100%

Only hard, sound pieces are counted — exclude soft/weathered material, mechanical breaks, and infilling. RQD is used directly in rock mass classification systems (RMR, Q-system).

**Key:** Distinguishing natural fractures from mechanical (drilling-induced) breaks is critical for accurate RQD. Mechanical breaks typically show fresh surfaces fitting tightly together, often perpendicular to the core axis.

## Core Logging

### Standard Logging Information
Per ASTM D2113 and ISRM guidelines, core logs record:
- Depth intervals (driller's depths)
- Core recovery (TCR, SCR, RQD) per run
- Rock type (lithology, mineralogy, texture, grain size)
- Weathering grade (fresh, slightly, moderately, highly, completely weathered, residual soil — per ISRM)
- Strength estimate (field methods: point load, Schmidt hammer, scratching with knife/pick)
- Discontinuity description: type (joint, bedding, foliation, fault, shear), roughness (JRC or ISRM scale), aperture, infilling (type, thickness), persistence (if observable), waviness
- Groundwater observations: loss zones, water inflow depths, artesian zones
- Core photographs: wet and dry, with scale bar and depth markers, color-calibrated

### Core Photography
Standard practice: photograph each core box wet (enhances color contrast and mineral identification). Include: box number, borehole ID, depth interval, scale bar, color reference card. Consistent lighting and camera angle across all boxes. Digital photography with systematic file naming.

### Core Storage
Core boxes stored in secure, covered facility. Protect from weather, freezing, and UV exposure. Maintain for project duration plus retention period (often years to decades for dam and tunnel projects). Core for laboratory testing should be sealed (wax, plastic wrap, or tubes) immediately after recovery to preserve moisture content.
