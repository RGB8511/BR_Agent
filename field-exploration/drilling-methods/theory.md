# Drilling Methods for Geotechnical Investigation

## Overview

The drilling method determines sample quality, logging accuracy, groundwater observations, and overall investigation quality. Method selection depends on soil/rock conditions, required sample types, depth, groundwater, access constraints, environmental considerations, and budget.

## Hollow-Stem Auger (HSA)

**Principle:** Continuous-flight auger with hollow center stem. Auger flights act as temporary casing while the hollow center allows sampling through the stem without removing the auger string.

**Procedure:** Advance auger flights to desired sample depth. Remove center plug/pilot bit. Lower sampler (split-spoon, Shelby tube) through hollow stem to sample undisturbed soil at the bottom of the borehole. Replace plug and advance to next sample interval.

**Advantages:** No drilling fluid required. Clean borehole for sampling. Fast in soft to medium soils. Groundwater observation possible when auger acts as temporary casing. Most common method for shallow geotechnical exploration in soil.

**Limitations:** Maximum practical depth ~30 m (100 ft) in favorable conditions, often less. Cannot penetrate hard rock, boulders, or very dense soils. Auger refusal in very dense/ceite soils, gravel, cobbles. Heaving sands below water table can plug the stem — may need to maintain water head inside stem.

**Sizes:** Common ID 83 mm (3.25 in) for SPT, 114 mm (4.5 in) for Shelby tubes. OD typically 150–300 mm (6–12 in).

## Mud Rotary

**Principle:** Rotating bit at bottom of drill string advances the borehole while drilling fluid (mud) circulates down the drill string and up the annulus, carrying cuttings to the surface and stabilizing the borehole wall.

**Drilling fluid:** Bentonite-water slurry (most common), polymer mud, or water. Mud weight and viscosity control borehole stability and cuttings transport. Hydrostatic pressure of mud column stabilizes borehole walls — critical in soft/loose soils and below water table.

**Advantages:** Can drill through virtually any soil or soft rock. Effective borehole stabilization in difficult ground. Continuous circulation cleans hole. Can reach great depths (100+ m). Relatively fast penetration rates in soil.

**Limitations:** Drilling mud contaminates samples (not suitable for thin-wall sampling without careful procedures). Mud disposal may be an environmental concern. Requires mud mixing/circulation equipment. Groundwater level observation requires well completion (mud plugs formation). Cuttings identification less precise than auger — relies on lag time calculation.

**Sampling:** Stop circulation, lower sampler to bottom of hole. Mud stabilizes borehole during sampling. Resume drilling after sample recovery.

## Air Rotary / Air Hammer (Down-the-Hole — DTH)

**Principle:** Compressed air circulates down drill string, powering a percussion hammer at the bit and carrying cuttings up the annulus. The hammer delivers high-frequency impact blows to the rock face.

**Advantages:** Very fast penetration in rock. Excellent for hard rock exploration. Air returns cuttings quickly — good chip logging possible. No contamination from drilling fluid. Water-bearing zones easily identified (water blows out with air).

**Limitations:** Not effective in soft soil (borehole collapses without casing). Requires casing through overburden. Noisy. Compressed air can cause heave in soft formations below casing. Not suitable for undisturbed sampling — produces only cuttings/chips.

**Variations:** Air rotary (tricone bit without hammer), air percussion/DTH (hammer at bit), air-mist (small amount of water/foam added for dust suppression and lubrication).

## Sonic / Vibratory Drilling

**Principle:** High-frequency (50–180 Hz) resonant vibration transmitted to drill string. The oscillating drill string fluidizes soil at the bit face and along the casing, allowing rapid penetration with minimal disturbance to surrounding ground.

**Advantages:** Continuous core sample in a core barrel (full-column recovery). Very fast — often 2–5× faster than conventional rotary. Minimal waste cuttings/spoils. Works in most soil types including cobbles and boulders. Excellent in environmental investigations (minimal cross-contamination between zones).

**Limitations:** Expensive equipment (specialized rigs). Sample disturbance from vibration — not suitable for high-quality undisturbed sampling for consolidation or triaxial testing. Limited penetration in hard rock (works in weathered rock). Core barrel diameter limits in-situ testing access.

**Core sizes:** Typically 75–200 mm (3–8 in) diameter continuous core.

## Cable Tool (Percussion)

**Principle:** Heavy bit on cable repeatedly lifted and dropped, breaking the formation. Cuttings mixed with water form slurry, removed by bailer. Casing driven ahead as hole deepens.

**Advantages:** Simple, rugged equipment. Excellent in boulder/cobble formations where rotary methods fail. Good water well construction method. Drives casing through difficult ground.

**Limitations:** Very slow compared to rotary methods. Not common for modern geotechnical exploration except in specific ground conditions (boulder fields). Limited sample quality.

## Direct Push (Geoprobe / Cone Penetration)

**Principle:** Hydraulic rams push sampling tools, probes, or sensors into the ground without rotation. No borehole created — tools are advanced and retracted through the same hole.

**Methods:** Macro-core sampling (continuous push tube), CPT/CPTu (instrumented cone), direct-push well installation, soil gas sampling, groundwater grab sampling.

**Advantages:** Very fast. Minimal investigation-derived waste. Small equipment footprint. Excellent for environmental site assessments and shallow geotechnical work. No drilling fluid.

**Limitations:** Depth limited to ~30 m in soft soils, much less in dense/hard ground. Cannot penetrate rock. Push refusal in dense gravel, cobbles, very stiff soil. Limited diameter (typically 25–50 mm sample).

## Casing Advancement Methods

**Principle:** Casing advanced simultaneously with or ahead of drilling, providing continuous borehole support in difficult ground (flowing sands, rubble fill, cobbles/boulders, weak/fractured rock).

**Systems:** Tubex (eccentric bit expands beyond casing OD), Symmetrix (concentric system), ODEX (similar to Tubex), casing with shoe bit. After casing advancement, standard sampling/coring tools work through the casing.

**Applications:** Overburden drilling to bedrock through difficult materials, landfill investigations, colluvium/talus slopes, alluvial deposits with cobbles/boulders.

## Wash Boring

**Principle:** Chopping bit on drill rods, with water pumped down rods and up annulus. Light, portable equipment. Historically very common; still used in some regions.

**Advantages:** Simple, lightweight, inexpensive equipment. Can be carried to remote/limited-access sites. Suitable for soft to medium soils.

**Limitations:** Slow. Cannot penetrate hard layers. Sample quality depends on interrupting wash to insert sampler. Largely replaced by HSA and rotary methods in developed countries.

## Rig Selection Considerations

Key factors: required depth, ground conditions (soil vs. rock, boulders, water table), sample types needed, site access (truck-mounted, track-mounted, skid-mounted, barge), environmental sensitivity, production requirements, and budget.

Most geotechnical programs use multiple methods: HSA in overburden soils, transitioning to rotary coring in rock. Air rotary for fast rock exploration when only cuttings are needed. Sonic for environmental or fast continuous profiling. Direct push for shallow, rapid assessments.
