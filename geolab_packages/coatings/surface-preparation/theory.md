# Surface Preparation

## The Critical Factor

Surface preparation is the single most important factor in coating performance. Industry data consistently shows that 60–80% of all coating failures trace back to inadequate surface preparation. No coating can outperform its substrate preparation.

## Initial Surface Condition (ISO 8501-1)

Steel surfaces are classified by their as-received condition:
- **A:** Steel surface largely covered with adhering mill scale, little or no rust
- **B:** Steel surface with beginning rust and from which mill scale has started to flake
- **C:** Steel surface on which mill scale has rusted away or can be scraped off, slight pitting
- **D:** Steel surface on which mill scale has rusted away, general pitting visible

Mill scale is the blue-black iron oxide formed during hot rolling. Although it appears protective, it is cathodic to steel — any break in mill scale creates intense localized corrosion at the exposed steel (small anode, large cathode).

## Abrasive Blast Cleaning

### Equipment
- **Conventional (dry) abrasive blasting:** Compressed air (690–1000 kPa / 100–150 psi) propels abrasive through nozzle. Most effective and most common method.
- **Centrifugal (wheel) blasting:** Mechanical impeller throws abrasive. Automated, high production for shop work (structural steel fabrication).
- **Wet abrasive blasting:** Abrasive mixed with water. Suppresses dust, removes soluble salts. Requires flash rust inhibitor.
- **Vacuum blasting:** Enclosed blast head with integral vacuum recovery. For confined spaces and lead paint removal.

### Abrasive Types
- **Expendable mineral:** Garnet (most common), copper slag, coal slag, staurolite. Single use for field work.
- **Recyclable metallic:** Steel shot (round — peening), steel grit (angular — cutting), chilled iron grit. For shop centrifugal blast.
- **Other:** Aluminum oxide (very hard, cutting), glass bead (peening, light cleaning), walnut shell/corn cob (soft stripping), dry ice (CO₂, no waste).

### Blast Profile (Anchor Pattern)

The roughness created by abrasive impact that provides mechanical adhesion for coatings. Measured as peak-to-valley height.

**Measurement methods:**
- **Replica tape (ASTM D4417 Method C):** Press-O-Film. Most common field method. Coarse (20–64 μm), X-coarse (38–115 μm).
- **Depth micrometer (Method B):** Direct measurement with pointed stylus.
- **Surface profile comparator (Method A):** Visual/tactile comparison to reference.
- **Digital profilometer:** Electronic measurement — most accurate.

**Profile requirements vary by coating:** IOZ needs 50–100 μm, epoxy primer 37–75 μm. Insufficient profile → poor adhesion. Excessive profile → peaks may telegraph through thin coats.

## Soluble Salt Contamination

Invisible chloride, sulfate, and nitrate salts on steel cause osmotic blistering under coatings. Salts create osmotic pressure gradient, drawing water through the coating.

**Testing methods:**
- **Bresle patch (ISO 8502-6):** Adhesive patch with distilled water chamber applied to surface. Water extracted and tested for conductivity.
- **Chloride ion detection (field kits):** Quantitative Cl⁻ measurement.

**Acceptance limits:** Maximum 3–7 μg/cm² chloride (varies by specification). Many marine/offshore specs: ≤ 3 μg/cm² NaCl equivalent. SSPC-SP 12 (water jetting) is effective at salt removal.

## Dust Assessment (ISO 8502-3)

After blasting, surface must be free of loose dust. Tested by pressing adhesive tape to surface and comparing to reference images. Rating 1 (dust particles barely visible) to 5 (heavy). Maximum acceptable: usually Rating 2–3.

## Alternative Methods

### Power Tool Cleaning (SSPC-SP 3, SP 11, SP 15)
- **SP 3:** Basic — remove loose rust, scale, paint. Not equivalent to blasting.
- **SP 11:** Power tool to bare metal. Rotary flap wheels, needle guns. For localized repair or where blasting is restricted.
- **SP 15:** Power tool to commercial grade. Intermediate between SP 3 and SP 11.

### Water Jetting (SSPC-SP 12/NACE No. 5)
High-pressure (> 170 MPa / 25,000 psi) or ultra-high-pressure (> 207 MPa / 30,000 psi) water.
- Removes soluble salts (advantage over dry blasting)
- Does not create anchor profile on smooth steel (disadvantage)
- Classified by degree of cleaning: WJ-1 through WJ-4 (cleanest to least)
- Excellent for maintenance where existing profile is adequate

### Chemical Treatment
- **Acid pickling:** Immersion in HCl or H₂SO₄ solution. Shop process for structural steel.
- **Phosphoric acid wash:** Converts rust to iron phosphate. Surface treatment, not true preparation.

## Environmental Considerations

### Containment
Dry blasting generates significant waste (spent abrasive + removed coating). Containment required for:
- Lead-containing paint (most pre-1980s industrial steel) — SSPC Guide 6
- Chromate primers
- Work over waterways
- Occupied facilities

Full containment with negative pressure ventilation, HEPA filtration, and proper waste disposal per EPA/OSHA requirements.

### Exposure Time
Blasted steel begins to rust within hours in humid conditions. Flash rust is accelerated by salts. Maximum exposure time before coating: typically 4–8 hours (many specs require same shift). Some IOZ specs allow up to 24 hours if no visible rust.
