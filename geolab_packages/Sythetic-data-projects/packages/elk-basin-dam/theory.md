# Elk Basin Dam — Safety Investigation, Monitoring & Remediation

## Project Description

Elk Basin Dam is a 145-foot-high concrete arch dam located on Elk Creek in Elmore County, Idaho, approximately 28 miles northeast of Mountain Home. The structure impounds Elk Basin Reservoir, which has a storage capacity of 18,500 acre-feet at the normal pool elevation of 4,218 feet above mean sea level. The dam is operated under FERC Project No. 4182 by the Elk Basin Irrigation District (EBID) and provides irrigation water to approximately 47,000 acres of agricultural land in the Snake River Plain.

Construction was completed between 1966 and 1968 by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) under a cooperative agreement with EBID. The dam has a crest length of 620 feet, a crest width of 12 feet, and a base thickness of 38 feet at the crown section. The arch is a variable-radius, variable-angle design with a maximum central angle of 82 degrees at the base.

Appurtenant structures include:

- **Powerhouse**: A 2.4 MW run-of-river powerhouse located at the right abutment toe, with a single 2.4 MW Francis turbine-generator unit. The powerhouse was added in 1974 and operates under a gross head of approximately 130 feet.
- **Outlet works**: A 48-inch diameter steel-lined conduit through the left abutment with a slide gate and fixed cone valve for reservoir drawdown and irrigation releases. Design capacity is 480 cfs at maximum head.
- **Spillway**: An ogee-crested overflow spillway at the left abutment with a crest length of 80 feet and design capacity of 12,400 cfs at the maximum water surface elevation of 4,221 feet. The spillway discharges into a concrete-lined chute and flip bucket, directing flow away from the left abutment foundation.
- **Embankment wings**: Compacted earth embankment sections extend from each abutment contact, totaling approximately 340 feet of embankment length. The left embankment wing ties into the spillway training wall; the right wing ties into the powerhouse intake structure.

The dam has performed satisfactorily for most of its operational life, but three interrelated safety concerns have emerged and escalated over the past decade: (1) progressive alkali-silica reaction (ASR) degradation of the arch concrete, (2) increasing seepage through the left abutment foundation, and (3) slow but persistent downstream displacement of the right abutment slope. These concerns were documented formally in the FERC Part 12D inspection of October 2020, which classified the project as requiring Priority Action, and have been the subject of an intensive geotechnical investigation and instrumentation program completed in 2025.

## Alkali-Silica Reaction Assessment

### Background and Chemistry

Alkali-silica reaction (ASR) is a deleterious chemical reaction between reactive silica minerals in the aggregate and alkali hydroxides (sodium, potassium, and calcium) in the cement pore solution. The reaction produces an expansive hydrophilic gel that absorbs pore water and swells, generating tensile stresses within the concrete matrix that exceed its tensile capacity, resulting in characteristic map cracking (also called pattern cracking or crazing) on exposed surfaces.

At Elk Basin Dam, the aggregate used in construction was sourced from a quarry approximately 4 miles upstream on Elk Creek. Petrographic examination of aggregate samples recovered from the quarry stockpile and from concrete cores (ASTM C856) confirmed the presence of reactive silica in the form of rhyolitic volcanic glass and microcrystalline quartz within the rhyolite lithology. These minerals are among the most reactive silica phases known; rhyolitic volcanic glass exhibits rapid gel formation with reactivity indices (as measured by ASTM C1260 mortar bar expansion) exceeding 0.40% at 14 days, well above the 0.20% threshold for potentially deleterious reaction.

The Portland cement used in original construction had a total alkali content (Na₂O equivalent) of 0.72% by weight of cement, measured from archived mill certificates. This exceeds the 0.60% limit recommended by ACI 201.2R for aggregates of moderate reactivity and is substantially above the 0.40% limit recommended for highly reactive aggregates such as those present at Elk Basin. The cement alkali content, combined with the highly reactive aggregate, created conditions highly favorable for ASR development over the dam's service life.

### Field Observations

Visual inspection of the downstream face was conducted from a boat and from access scaffolding during the 2020 FERC inspection and again during the 2025 investigation. Observations include:

- **Map cracking extent**: Map cracking is present on approximately 60% of the downstream face, concentrated in the lower two-thirds of the arch below elevation 4,180 feet. Crack widths range from hairline (< 0.1 mm) in the upper transition zones to 0.5–1.2 mm in the most severely affected areas near the base. Crack spacing in the most affected panels averages 50–75 mm, consistent with internal ASR expansion.
- **Gel exudation**: Silica gel exudation has been observed at 25 distinct locations on the downstream face. Gel is white to cream-colored, translucent to opaque, and gelatinous to waxy in texture depending on age and hydration state. Active seepage at three of the gel exudation locations indicates open crack connectivity from the exposed face to interior moisture sources.
- **Surface deposits**: Calcium carbonate efflorescence is present over approximately 35% of the downstream face, overlying or interspersed with gel deposits, indicating long-term moisture movement through the concrete.
- **Geometric distortion**: Survey of the downstream face reveals a maximum outward bulge of 0.8 inches at the arch crown (elevation 4,198 feet), measured relative to the as-built alignment. This bulge has increased by 0.15 inches since the 2010 survey.

### Core Investigation and Petrographic Analysis

NX-diameter (54 mm) cores were drilled through the arch at the crown section, penetrating the full 22-foot arch thickness. Four ASR severity zones were identified based on field logging and confirmed by petrographic analysis (ASTM C856) conducted at a specialized concrete materials laboratory:

**Zone 1 — Severely Affected (0–4 feet from downstream face)**: Abundant reactive silica gel filling aggregate cracks, cement paste microcracks, and bleed channels. Significant ettringite deposits in air voids and cracks. Aggregate particles show peripheral cracking and gel rims. Cement paste exhibits extensive microcracking with crack widths up to 80 microns. ASR reaction rims visible around most reactive aggregate particles.

**Zone 2 — Moderately Affected (4–8 feet from downstream face)**: Moderate amounts of reactive silica gel, primarily in aggregate cracks. Cement paste microcracking present but less pervasive. Ettringite deposits in approximately 40% of air voids. Some aggregate particles intact.

**Zone 3 — Mildly Affected (8–16 feet from downstream face)**: Occasional gel in aggregate cracks and at aggregate-paste interfaces. Minor cement paste microcracking. Ettringite in less than 20% of air voids. Most aggregate particles intact.

**Zone 4 — Sound Interior (16–22 feet, upstream face)**: No ASR gel observed. Cement paste intact with minimal microcracking. Good aggregate-paste bond. Original pore structure largely preserved.

The ASR penetration front has been estimated at 4–6 feet from the downstream face based on petrographic zonation and is advancing at an estimated rate of 0.3–0.5 feet per decade based on comparison with cores taken in 1998 and 2012. This rate is consistent with the available moisture conditions (downstream face is exposed to precipitation and humidity; upstream face is submerged, limiting moisture availability for gel swelling).

### Mechanical Property Degradation

Compressive and tensile strength testing was performed on core specimens prepared in accordance with ASTM C42. Results by ASR severity zone:

**Zone 1 (Severely Affected)**: Mean compressive strength f'c = 4,850 psi, representing a 28% reduction from the as-built design strength of 6,750 psi specified by USBR. Mean indirect tensile (split cylinder, ASTM C496) strength f't = 320 psi. UCS/ITS ratio = 15.2, consistent with moderate microcracking (intact concrete typically exhibits ratios of 10–13).

**Zones 2 and 3 (Moderately to Mildly Affected)**: Gradational strength recovery. Zone 2 mean compressive = 5,820 psi; Zone 3 mean compressive = 6,340 psi.

**Zone 4 (Sound Interior)**: Mean compressive strength = 6,720–6,890 psi (range across 5 specimens), consistent with the original design specification. Mean indirect tensile = 510 psi. UCS/ITS ratio = 13.3.

The strength reduction in the severe zone (26–28% in compression, 37% in tension) represents a significant degradation for a structure relying on arch action, which develops compressive ring stress under reservoir loading. Tensile stresses arise at the abutment contacts and at the upstream face under temperature contraction and seismic loading. The residual tensile capacity of 320 psi in the severe zone is marginal relative to the calculated tensile demand of 250–350 psi under the design loading combinations evaluated in the 2020 FERC inspection FEM analysis.

Carbonation depth, measured by phenolphthalein indicator, averages 0.8 inches from the downstream face. Carbonation has depleted the alkalinity of the outer concrete layer, which has locally arrested ASR gel formation in the outermost inch but has not arrested progression at depth.

## Left Abutment Seepage Investigation

### Seepage History

The left abutment seepage monitoring program has been in operation since initial filling in 1969. Seepage is collected in a drainage gallery within the left abutment and measured at a 90-degree V-notch weir (Weir 1) installed in 1969. A second weir (Weir 2) was installed in 1995 to segregate arch gallery drainage from abutment drainage.

Baseline seepage measured during the first operational season (1969–1971) was approximately 8 gallons per minute (gpm) at normal pool elevation. Seepage remained relatively stable at 7–10 gpm through 1990. A gradual upward trend began in the early 1990s, accelerating after 1998. By the time of the 2020 FERC Part 12D inspection, left abutment seepage had reached 33.7 gpm at normal pool — a 321% increase from the 1969 baseline measured over 51 years, with a mean linear rate of approximately 0.5 gpm per year over the full record period and an accelerating rate of 1.5 gpm per year over the most recent decade (2010–2020).

Seepage is turbid on approximately 15% of readings, suggesting fines transport and potential internal erosion within the flow path. Turbidity events are correlated with rapid reservoir drawdown rather than high pool elevations, suggesting that hydraulic gradients reverse during drawdown and mobilize fines that have been deposited during high-pool periods.

### Subsurface Investigation of Flow Path

The 2025 geotechnical investigation drilled three borings (B-1, B-2, B-3) in the left abutment to characterize the seepage flow path and assess the condition of the existing grout curtain. Investigation methods included:

- **Rotary coring** with NX triple-tube core barrel to obtain rock quality designation (RQD) and permeability samples
- **Packer permeability tests** at 10-foot intervals to measure in-situ hydraulic conductivity
- **Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT)** to map zones of high moisture content and potential flow paths between borings
- **Borehole televiewer logging** to characterize fracture orientation, aperture, and infilling

Key findings:

**Embankment clay core**: The clay core was intersected in boring B-1 at its design location and was found to be intact and continuous. Laboratory permeability (falling head, ASTM D5084) of trimmed core specimens yielded k = 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm/s, consistent with the as-built specification and well within the range for a competent low-permeability barrier. No piping features, sinkholes, or internal erosion were observed in the core. The clay core is not the source of increasing seepage.

**Weathered granodiorite contact zone**: The left abutment foundation consists of granodiorite bedrock exhibiting a weathering profile typical of glacially scoured crystalline rock. The upper 25–40 feet of rock is classified as highly weathered (Grade IV–V per ISRM classification), with RQD averaging 18% in the weathered zone. Packer tests in the weathered zone yielded hydraulic conductivities of approximately 10⁻⁴ cm/s (10–50 Lugeons), approximately three orders of magnitude higher than the clay core.

**Grout curtain deterioration**: The original grout curtain, installed in 1967–1968, was designed to penetrate 30 feet below the contact into fresh rock. Borehole televiewer logs and grout take records from the 2025 supplemental grouting investigation reveal that the curtain is discontinuous in the weathered zone, with grout takes during test grouting of 15–45 Lugeons indicating effective hydraulic connections that were not intercepted by the original curtain. ERT results show a high-conductivity zone trending approximately N15°W through the weathered granodiorite at the abutment-embankment contact, consistent with the principal seepage path.

**Flow path interpretation**: The combined evidence indicates that increased seepage is flowing through the weathered granodiorite in the upper left abutment foundation, bypassing the grout curtain, and discharging into the drainage gallery. The embankment clay core is intact and functioning as designed; the seepage increase is entirely attributable to progressive deterioration or bypass of the foundation grout curtain within the weathered rock mass.

### Piezometric Response

Vibrating wire piezometer P-3, installed in 1988 in the left abutment at elevation 4,168 feet (50 feet below the embankment crest), monitors pore pressure within the weathered granodiorite. P-3 readings have increased from a 2010 baseline of 10.5 psi to 18.0 psi in 2025, a 71% increase over 15 years. The increase is correlated with rising seepage rates and with periods of sustained high reservoir levels, indicating that pore pressures in the weathered zone are responding to reservoir loading through the deteriorating grout curtain. The rate of pressure increase (approximately 0.5 psi per year) has been relatively constant over the monitoring period, suggesting a gradual expansion of the effective flow area through the weathered rock.

Piezometer P-1, installed in the embankment clay core at elevation 4,180 feet, shows pore pressures consistent with the phreatic surface expected for a properly functioning clay core: steady-state values of 4.2–5.8 psi, with small seasonal fluctuations tracking reservoir level changes with a 2–4 week lag. The P-1 response confirms that the clay core is providing effective seepage control within the embankment.

## Right Abutment Displacement

### Displacement History

The right abutment slope has been monitored for lateral movement since 1995 using a network of survey monuments installed on the crest of the right embankment wing and on the powerhouse structure. Four monuments (RA-1 through RA-4) provide a longitudinal profile of crest displacement from the arch contact to the downstream toe.

Cumulative downstream displacement at monument RA-2 (nearest the arch contact) has reached 3.07 inches since 1995, measured over 30 years of monitoring. The displacement record is strikingly linear, with a mean rate of 0.20 inches per year that has remained essentially constant across the full monitoring period. There has been no acceleration in response to wet years (high reservoir) or dry years (low pool), and no step increases following seismic events, both of which would be expected if the displacement were associated with progressive slope failure or seismically triggered movement.

Displacement diminishes with distance from the arch: RA-3 has accumulated 2.21 inches (0.14 in/yr) and RA-4 has accumulated 1.05 inches (0.07 in/yr). Monument RA-1, located on the concrete powerhouse structure within the rock zone, shows no measurable displacement, confirming that movement is limited to the embankment and glacial till material on the right abutment slope and does not extend into the competent rock foundation.

### Geological and Geotechnical Context

The right abutment slope is underlain by a deposit of glacial till (basal till) deposited during the Pleistocene glaciation of the Elk Creek drainage. The till is overridden by the right embankment wing and is exposed on the natural slope below and downstream of the powerhouse. Till thickness is 30–55 feet based on boring B-4 and surface exposures, overlying fresh granodiorite bedrock.

Glacial till at this site is classified as GC (clayey gravel) per the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS). Index properties indicate a plastic fine fraction with liquid limit LL = 26–28 and plasticity index PI = 11–12, falling in the CL-ML range for the fines component. The matrix-supported fabric of the till (fines content 28–35%) means the engineering behavior is governed by the fine-grained matrix rather than the gravel skeleton.

Triaxial consolidated-undrained (CU) testing with pore pressure measurement (ASTM D4767) was performed on one undisturbed sample of glacial till (specimen GT-1). Results yielded effective stress friction angle φ' = 34° and effective cohesion c' = 0 (or a very small value of approximately 50 psf, considered negligible for long-term stability analysis). The relatively high friction angle reflects the granular component of the till fabric.

Creep behavior is a well-documented characteristic of glacially overconsolidated soils subjected to sustained shear stresses below the peak strength envelope. Secondary compression (creep) at constant effective stress occurs due to time-dependent rearrangement of the clay fabric. The constant displacement rate observed at Elk Basin — 0.20 inches per year with no trend toward acceleration or deceleration — is the defining diagnostic characteristic of creep rather than progressive failure: progressive failure exhibits an accelerating rate as the soil strength degrades toward residual values, whereas creep exhibits a constant or slowly decelerating rate at stresses that remain below peak strength.

### Stability Analysis

The right abutment slope stability was analyzed using Spencer's method of slices (which satisfies both force and moment equilibrium) with the material properties summarized below:

| Material | c' (psf) | φ' (degrees) | γ (pcf) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gravel shell (GP-GM) | 0 | 38 | 135 |
| Clay core (CL) | 600 | 26 | 122 |
| Glacial till (GC) | 0 | 34 | 130 |
| Weathered granodiorite | 200 | 38 | 155 |

**Static analysis (Spencer's method)**: The critical slip surface was identified at the base of the glacial till, at the till–bedrock interface, consistent with the observed displacement pattern. Factor of safety FS = 1.42 under static loading with reservoir at normal pool. The static FS exceeds the FERC minimum of 1.5 for normal loading conditions, but the margin is reduced relative to the design FS of 1.8 assumed in original design documentation.

**Pseudo-static seismic analysis**: Applying a horizontal seismic coefficient k_h = 0.12 (corresponding to the 10% probability of exceedance in 50-year ground motion for the site per USGS PSHA mapping), the pseudo-static factor of safety is FS = 1.08. This marginally exceeds 1.0 but is below the FERC guideline of 1.1–1.2 for the seismic load combination, indicating that permanent displacement analysis (Newmark sliding block method) is warranted.

**Newmark displacement analysis**: The yield acceleration (acceleration at which FS = 1.0) was determined from the pseudo-static analysis as a_y = 0.15 g. Newmark sliding block integration using the USGS Hazus ground motion suite for the site location yields expected permanent displacement of 1.8–3.5 inches (mean 2.4 inches) for the 10%/50-year ground motion. This level of displacement is considered tolerable for an embankment wing (no dam breach mechanism) but warrants monitoring for post-earthquake assessment.

**Pore pressure assessment**: Piezometers within the right abutment embankment and till (P-4, P-5, installed 2025) show pore pressure ratios (r_u = u/γ_s z) of 0.12–0.18, consistent with steady-state seepage conditions and well below the levels that would indicate excess pore pressure generation or artesian conditions. The stable pore pressures confirm that the observed displacement is not associated with pore pressure-driven failure and support the creep interpretation.

## Subsurface Conditions

### Embankment Zones

The embankment sections flanking the arch dam were designed by USBR as a zoned earth fill with the following zones:

**Gravel shell (Zones 1 and 3)**: Random gravel and cobble fill sourced from the Elk Creek alluvium, classified as GP-GM (poorly graded gravel with silt and sand). Standard penetration test (SPT) N-values of 32–45 blows per foot (corrected for overburden and equipment) indicate dense to very dense condition. The shell provides structural stability and drainage.

**Clay core (Zone 2)**: Compacted silty clay sourced from a borrow area approximately 1.5 miles west of the dam site, classified as CL (lean clay). Atterberg limits: liquid limit LL = 39–44, plasticity index PI = 19–21. Moisture content at placement was 1–3% wet of optimum. Compacted dry density averages 105.2 pcf (98.4% relative compaction based on modified Proctor maximum dry density of 106.9 pcf). Laboratory permeability k = 2.8 × 10⁻⁷ cm/s confirms effective seepage control.

**Filter and drain (Zone 4)**: Granular filter material enveloping the clay core transitions, designed to prevent piping of core material into the gravel shell. Laboratory gradation confirms filter criteria (D15_filter / D85_core < 5, D15_filter / D15_core > 5) are met. Drain outlets function properly based on observed flow rates.

### Foundation Rock

**Weathered granodiorite**: The upper foundation zone, extending 25–40 feet below original ground surface, consists of highly to completely weathered granodiorite (ISRM Grades IV–V). Core recovery is 45–65%; RQD averages 18%. The weathered material is friable to crumbly, with clay-filled joint surfaces and a soil-like matrix in the most weathered intervals. Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of hand-trimmed specimens ranges from 1,280 to 4,100 psi, reflecting high variability within the weathered zone. In-situ hydraulic conductivity k ≈ 10⁻⁴ cm/s (packer tests, equivalent to 10–50 Lugeons).

**Fresh granodiorite**: Below the weathering front, the granodiorite is fresh to slightly weathered (ISRM Grades I–II). Core recovery is 88–97%; RQD averages 72–85%. UCS of NX cores ranges from 18,400 to 25,600 psi with a mean of 22,467 psi across 9 specimens. The Young's modulus E50 averages 8.4 × 10⁶ psi and the Poisson's ratio averages 0.24. Joint spacing averages 1.2 feet with three dominant joint sets: a sub-horizontal set (dip 8–15°), a steeply dipping set striking approximately N30°E (dip 70–80° SE), and a less-prominent cross-joint set striking N60°W. In-situ hydraulic conductivity in the fresh rock is 0.1–0.8 Lugeons (k ≈ 10⁻⁷ cm/s), effectively impermeable for practical purposes.

**Glacial till**: The right abutment Pleistocene basal till, described above under the displacement section. Additional index testing on right abutment till boring specimens: LL = 26–28, PL = 15–16, PI = 11–12, moisture content 12–16% (slightly above optimum), very dense with SPT N-values > 50 (refusal) in the deepest till intervals. Gradation: gravel 38–42%, sand 22–28%, fines (silt + clay) 31–38%. The high gravel content combined with a plastic fine matrix is characteristic of basal (lodgement) till, which has been overconsolidated by glacial overburden pressures estimated at 600–900 psf based on regional ice thickness reconstructions.

## Concrete Core Analysis

### Coring Program

NX-diameter (54 mm) continuous rotary cores were drilled through the arch at the crown section in November 2025. Core holes were drilled vertically downward from the dam crest, penetrating the full 22-foot arch thickness. A total of 66 feet of core was recovered from three adjacent holes (Crown-1, Crown-2, Crown-3), with mean core recovery of 91% for the sound interior and 74% for the severely deteriorated Zone 1.

Core logging followed ASTM C42 procedures for sampling and testing hardened concrete. Cores were photographed at 1:1 scale, logged for fractures, gel exudation, coarse aggregate lithology, and visible ASR features, and then cut into test specimens for mechanical testing and petrographic analysis.

### ASR Severity Zone Classification

Zone boundaries were established by consensus of field core logging, laboratory petrographic analysis (ASTM C856), and mechanical test results. The four zones and their properties are summarized as follows:

**Zone 1 — Severe (0–4 ft from downstream face)**
- UCS range: 4,450–5,220 psi; mean 4,850 psi
- Split tensile (ITS): 295–345 psi; mean 320 psi
- UCS/ITS ratio: 15.1 (elevated, indicating microcracking)
- Petrographic features: abundant gel, pervasive microcracking, ettringite in > 80% of voids
- Carbonation depth from downstream face: 0.8 inches (phenolphthalein)

**Zone 2 — Moderate (4–8 ft)**
- UCS range: 5,510–6,120 psi; mean 5,820 psi
- Split tensile: 420–455 psi; mean 438 psi
- Petrographic features: moderate gel, moderate microcracking, ettringite in 30–50% of voids

**Zone 3 — Mild (8–16 ft)**
- UCS range: 6,010–6,680 psi; mean 6,340 psi
- Split tensile: 470–490 psi; mean 480 psi
- Petrographic features: occasional gel, minor microcracking, ettringite in < 20% of voids

**Zone 4 — Sound (16–22 ft, upstream face)**
- UCS range: 6,720–6,890 psi; mean 6,810 psi
- Split tensile: 500–520 psi; mean 510 psi
- UCS/ITS ratio: 13.3 (normal for concrete)
- Petrographic features: no ASR gel, intact paste, good aggregate bond

### Structural Implications

The ASR degradation pattern — severe at the downstream (free) face, sound at the upstream (submerged) face — reflects the moisture distribution within the arch. ASR gel expansion requires absorbed water; the upstream face, continuously submerged, lacks the moisture gradient necessary for gel swelling and expansion. The downstream face, subject to precipitation, surface runoff, and humidity cycles, provides the moisture needed for ongoing gel expansion and cracking.

The structural consequence is that the downstream fiber of the arch has reduced strength and increased cracking, while the upstream fiber remains near-original specification. Under arch compression (the dominant stress state at reservoir loading), this asymmetry is partly self-compensating because the dominant compressive stress is oriented circumferentially (along the arch), and the severely degraded zone represents only the outer 18% of the arch thickness. However, under tensile loading (temperature contraction, seismic loading), the degraded downstream zone is the critical element, and its residual tensile capacity of 320 psi is marginal relative to calculated demands.

## Instrumentation Program

### Original Instrumentation (1968)

The original instrumentation suite installed during construction included:

- **Seepage weirs**: Two 90-degree V-notch weirs (Weir 1 in left abutment gallery, Weir 2 added 1995 in arch gallery) for continuous seepage measurement
- **Foundation piezometers**: 6 open-standpipe piezometers (P-1 through P-6) in embankment and foundation; installed with pneumatic-pressure conversion tubes in 1978
- **Embankment piezometers**: 4 pneumatic piezometers within the compacted fill zones
- **Survey monuments**: 12 stainless steel monuments on the dam crest and downstream face for geodetic survey
- **Plumblines**: 2 inverted plumblines in the arch gallery measuring lateral and longitudinal arch displacement at 3 elevations each
- **Joint meters**: 8 joint meters spanning construction joints in the arch
- **Strain meters**: 4 embedded vibrating wire strain meters near the abutment contacts (installed 2003)

Of the 46 original 1968 instruments, 30 (65%) remain functional as of 2025. Six piezometers have been abandoned due to casing corrosion or blockage. Four survey monuments have been disturbed by maintenance activities and replaced. The two plumblines are functional and provide the most reliable long-term geometric data for the arch.

### 2025 Instrumentation Additions

Following the 2025 investigation, the following new instruments were installed:

- **Vibrating wire piezometers**: 4 new VW piezometers (P-7 through P-10) installed in investigation borings B-1 through B-4, replacing abandoned standpipe piezometers and providing higher-accuracy pore pressure measurement with electronic data logging
- **Inclinometer**: One inclinometer casing installed in boring B-5 (right abutment, through the till deposit to bedrock) for high-accuracy lateral displacement profiling; initial readings taken November 2025 establish baseline for future monitoring
- **Automated data acquisition**: A Campbell Scientific CR6 datalogger with cellular telemetry was installed to collect VW piezometer readings at 4-hour intervals and transmit data to EBID operations; manual readings continue for weirs and survey monuments

### Data Continuity and Quality

Long-term monitoring data continuity at Elk Basin is considered good by dam safety standards. The seepage record is complete from 1969 to present with only a 6-month gap in 1983 (weir repair). The crest survey record is complete from 1968 to present at annual frequency. Piezometer records have gaps associated with instrument repairs but sufficient continuity to establish trends.

Data quality for the most recent decade (2015–2025) is high, with no data gaps in the seepage or displacement records and improved precision from upgraded measurement equipment.

## Ten-Year Monitoring Trends (2015–2025)

The decade from 2015 to 2025 represents the best-documented period in the dam's monitoring history and forms the primary basis for the current safety assessment.

### Seepage Trend

Left abutment total seepage (Weir 1 + Weir 2) increased from 18.0 gpm in 2015 to 33.7 gpm in 2025, an absolute increase of 15.7 gpm or 87% over the decade. The mean linear rate of increase over this period is 1.57 gpm per year, significantly higher than the long-term rate of 0.5 gpm per year, indicating accelerating deterioration of the foundation grout curtain.

Weir 2 (arch gallery drainage) shows a much smaller trend, increasing from 1.2 gpm to 1.8 gpm over the same period (50% increase, 0.06 gpm/yr). This confirms that the seepage increase is concentrated in the left abutment foundation drainage rather than in seepage through the arch concrete itself.

Right abutment seepage (Weir 3, installed 2001) has shown no significant trend, averaging 2.1 gpm with seasonal variation of ±0.4 gpm correlating with reservoir level.

### Piezometric Trend

P-3 (left abutment weathered granodiorite) has increased from 10.5 psi in 2015 to 18.0 psi in 2025, an increase of 7.5 psi (71%) over the decade or approximately 0.75 psi per year.

P-1 (embankment clay core) readings are stable, varying seasonally between 4.2 and 5.8 psi with no multi-year trend, confirming clay core integrity.

Right abutment piezometers P-4 and P-5 (installed 2025) show stable pore pressures of 4.8–6.2 psi consistent with normal embankment seepage conditions; the short record (< 1 year) precludes trend analysis.

### Displacement Trend

Right abutment crest monuments show a continued constant displacement rate of 0.20 inches per year at RA-2, with no acceleration detectable over the 30-year record. The 2025 reading of 3.07 inches cumulative displacement at RA-2 since 1995 establishes the displacement record at the 30-year mark.

The dam arch plumblines show a very slow downstream drift of 0.1 mm per year at the midheight plumbline (elevation 4,188 feet), with no acceleration. This drift is consistent with long-term creep of the arch foundation and is within the normal range for concrete arch dams of this age and height.

### Synthesis

The monitoring trend analysis identifies left abutment seepage as the most actively evolving condition and the primary safety concern requiring near-term remediation. The rate of seepage increase (87% over 10 years, 1.57 gpm/yr) and the correlated piezometric increase in the weathered foundation zone indicate progressive enlargement of the seepage flow path. If this trend continues at the current rate, seepage would reach approximately 50 gpm within 10 years. At that level, the risk of internal erosion and piping in the weathered granodiorite becomes a credible failure mode.

Right abutment displacement continues at a constant rate consistent with creep; no change in safety status is indicated, but continued annual monitoring is essential to detect any rate change that might signal a transition from creep to progressive failure.

ASR continues to advance at a slow rate (0.3–0.5 ft/decade penetration front advance) and does not represent an imminent safety threat, but requires longer-term management to prevent continued strength reduction in the arch concrete.

## Remediation Strategy

The 2025 Remediation Alternatives Evaluation (MWDE, November 2025) evaluated four remediation strategies based on the investigation findings:

### Alternative 1: Supplemental Grouting of Left Abutment (Recommended — High Priority)

Estimated cost: $180,000–$250,000. Scope includes drilling 8–12 grout holes (NX diameter, 40-foot depth) in a fan pattern from the left abutment drainage gallery, targeting the weathered granodiorite zone identified by ERT as the primary seepage path. Grout mix: stable cement-bentonite grout with water:cement ratio 0.6:1.0 by weight, with silica fume addition (8% by weight of cement) for improved durability and low permeability. Injection pressure limited per the grouting pressure criterion (P_max = 0.5 × depth in psi/ft) to prevent hydrofracture of the existing grout curtain. Target post-grouting Lugeon values ≤ 3 Lugeons in the treated zone. This alternative directly addresses the primary safety concern (increasing seepage) and provides the highest risk reduction per dollar spent.

Timing: Immediate implementation recommended, completing construction in Year 1 (2026–2027). Grouting to be performed at low pool (< 4,190 feet) to minimize grout loss to open fractures under high hydraulic gradient.

### Alternative 2: Lithium Treatment for ASR Mitigation (Recommended — Medium Priority)

Estimated cost: $120,000–$180,000. Scope includes application of lithium nitrate solution (30% LiNO₃) to the downstream face of the arch by pressure injection into drilled ports at 18-inch spacing. Lithium ions suppress ASR gel expansion by replacing sodium and potassium ions at the silica reaction sites, forming a less expansive lithium-silicate gel. Treatment effectiveness is supported by laboratory and field studies (Diamond, 2000; Stanton et al., 2002) for concrete in early-to-moderate ASR stages. The sound interior (Zone 4) and the mildly affected Zone 3 are expected to benefit most; the severely degraded Zone 1 is unlikely to recover mechanical properties but treatment may arrest further deterioration.

Timing: Defer to Year 2 (2027–2028) pending confirmation of grouting effectiveness. Combine with protective coating application to the downstream face to reduce moisture infiltration and slow ASR progression.

### Alternative 3: Finite Element Model Update (Recommended — Medium Priority)

Estimated cost: $60,000–$90,000. Scope includes development of an updated three-dimensional FEM of the arch dam incorporating measured material properties from the 2025 investigation (reduced properties for Zone 1, measured moduli), updated foundation stiffness from the boring program, and revised loading including current reservoir operations and updated seismic hazard. The 2020 FERC inspection relied on the 1986 USBR FEM model with assumed material properties; the updated model will provide a current assessment of arch stresses and abutment contact forces under various loading combinations. Results will inform the need for structural intervention (e.g., buttress, epoxy injection) and will support FERC reporting requirements.

Timing: Initiate in Year 1 concurrent with grouting design; complete by Year 2.

### Alternative 4: Contingency Buttress (Contingency — Defer)

Estimated cost: $350,000–$500,000. A downstream concrete buttress at the arch crown, founded on competent granodiorite, would provide supplemental lateral support to the arch if FEM analysis indicates that ASR-induced strength reduction has compromised the structural reserve. This alternative is considered contingent on FEM results and is not recommended for immediate implementation. If the updated FEM confirms adequate structural margins with measured material properties, the buttress is unnecessary and should be deferred indefinitely.

### Enhanced Monitoring (All Alternatives)

Continued annual crest surveys, quarterly seepage measurements, monthly P-3 piezometer readings, and annual inclinometer surveys at B-5. If grouting is successful and seepage decreases to < 15 gpm, monitoring frequency may be reduced after 5 years of stable readings. Annual reporting to FERC under 18 CFR Part 12 is required.

## Slope Stability Analysis

### Analysis Method and Software

Slope stability analyses were performed using Spencer's method (Spencer, 1967), which satisfies both global moment equilibrium and global force equilibrium for the interslice forces. Spencer's method is recommended by FERC Engineering Guidelines Chapter 11 for critical dam foundation slopes and is the most appropriate limit-equilibrium method for this application. Analyses were performed using Rocscience Slide2 v9.0.

### Material Properties

Material properties used in stability analysis are summarized in the following sections, drawn from the 2025 laboratory testing program:

**Gravel shell (GP-GM)**: Effective friction angle φ' = 38°, effective cohesion c' = 0, total unit weight γ = 135 pcf. Properties based on SPT correlations (N = 32–45) and direct shear tests on reconstituted specimens. No fines cohesion assumed for conservative analysis.

**Clay core (CL)**: Effective friction angle φ' = 26°, effective cohesion c' = 600 psf, total unit weight γ = 122 pcf. Properties from CU triaxial tests on undisturbed tube samples from boring B-1. Shear envelope is linear over the stress range of interest (50–300 psf normal effective stress).

**Glacial till (GC)**: Effective friction angle φ' = 34°, effective cohesion c' = 0 (conservative; actual c' ≈ 50 psf from triaxial test but neglected for long-term analysis), total unit weight γ = 130 pcf. Properties from CU triaxial test on undisturbed sample GT-1.

**Weathered granodiorite**: Effective friction angle φ' = 38°, effective cohesion c' = 200 psf, total unit weight γ = 155 pcf. Properties estimated from rock mass classification (RMR = 35–45 for weathered zone) using Hoek-Brown to Mohr-Coulomb conversion at the stress level of interest.

**Fresh granodiorite**: Assigned high strength (φ' = 55°, c' = 5,000 psf) consistent with the observed UCS of 18,400–25,600 psi; this material does not govern any analyzed failure surface.

### Static Analysis Results

The critical failure surface for the static case was identified by grid-of-centers search with circular and non-circular surfaces. The governing surface is a composite failure plane following the base of the glacial till deposit (at the till–weathered rock interface) for most of its length, with a circular arc through the embankment fill at each end. This surface is consistent with the field observation of displacement concentrated in the glacial till zone.

Spencer's method FS (static, normal pool) = **1.42**

The FERC minimum factor of safety for the normal loading condition (normal pool, steady-state seepage) is 1.5 per FERC Engineering Guidelines Chapter 11, Appendix E. The computed FS of 1.42 is slightly below this threshold, which is a basis for the enhanced monitoring program and the recommendation for periodic stability re-evaluation.

### Seismic Analysis — Pseudo-Static

The horizontal seismic coefficient k_h = 0.12 was derived from the USGS National Seismic Hazard Model 2018 for the site coordinates (43.52°N, 115.88°W), corresponding to the peak ground acceleration at 10% probability of exceedance in 50 years (475-year return period) divided by 2 as per FERC guidance for pseudo-static analysis of embankment slopes.

Pseudo-static Spencer's method FS (k_h = 0.12, reservoir at normal pool) = **1.08**

This value exceeds 1.0 (no slope failure) but falls below the FERC guideline of 1.1–1.2 for seismic loading with return period ≥ 475 years. Per FERC Engineering Guidelines, when pseudo-static FS < 1.2, the Newmark method is required to assess acceptability of expected permanent displacement.

### Seismic Analysis — Newmark Method

The Newmark sliding block method treats the potential failure mass as a rigid block sliding on the critical slip surface. The yield acceleration a_y = k_y × g is the acceleration at which the FS equals 1.0. From the pseudo-static analysis, k_y = 0.15 (dimensionless) or a_y = 0.15 g.

Integration of the Newmark equation over the USGS Hazus ground motion suite for the site location (10 recordings scaled to the 10%/50-year hazard level) yields permanent displacement estimates:

- Median expected displacement: 2.4 inches
- Range: 1.8–3.5 inches
- 84th percentile displacement: 3.0 inches

FERC Engineering Guidelines indicate that permanent embankment displacements up to 12 inches are generally tolerable for embankment wing sections where breach of the primary water barrier (arch dam) is not a consequence. The computed displacement of 2.4 inches (median) is well within this tolerance, supporting the conclusion that the right abutment displacement, while notable, does not represent a safety-of-dam concern under the design seismic loading.

Post-earthquake inspection and piezometer reading would be required following any M ≥ 4.5 earthquake within 25 miles of the site to assess actual displacement and verify that pore pressures remain within normal bounds.
