# Mineralogy & Clay Behavior

## Clay Mineral Structures

Clay minerals are phyllosilicates (sheet silicates) built from two basic structural units: the silica tetrahedron and the alumina/magnesia octahedron. These units combine into sheets, and the stacking arrangement defines the mineral type.

### Kaolinite (1:1 mineral)
- Structure: One tetrahedral sheet bonded to one octahedral sheet (TO)
- Layer spacing: ~7.2 Å (fixed — does not expand)
- Bonding: Hydrogen bonds between layers — moderately strong
- CEC: 3–15 meq/100g (low)
- Engineering behavior: Low plasticity, low compressibility, low swelling. Relatively "good" clay for construction.
- Typical LL: 30–75%, PI: 10–40%
- Particle size: Relatively large, well-defined hexagonal plates ~0.1–4 μm

### Illite (2:1 non-expanding)
- Structure: One octahedral sheet between two tetrahedral sheets (TOT)
- Layer spacing: ~10 Å (fixed — K⁺ ions between layers prevent expansion)
- Bonding: Potassium ions bridge between layers — strong
- CEC: 10–40 meq/100g (moderate)
- Engineering behavior: Moderate plasticity and compressibility. Most common clay mineral in temperate soils.
- Typical LL: 60–120%, PI: 30–60%

### Montmorillonite / Smectite (2:1 expanding)
- Structure: TOT like illite but weak bonding between layers
- Layer spacing: Variable 9.6–21+ Å — expands when hydrated
- Bonding: Weak van der Waals forces + exchangeable cations (Na⁺, Ca²⁺)
- CEC: 80–150 meq/100g (very high)
- Engineering behavior: Very high plasticity, extreme swelling, very low permeability. Most problematic clay mineral.
- Na-montmorillonite: Extreme swelling (bentonite). LL can exceed 700%.
- Ca-montmorillonite: Less swelling than Na form but still highly plastic.
- Typical LL: 100–900%, PI: 50–600%

### Chlorite (2:1:1)
- Structure: TOT with brucite interlayer
- Properties intermediate between illite and montmorillonite
- CEC: 10–40 meq/100g
- Common in metamorphic rocks

## Double-Layer Theory

The clay particle surface carries a net negative charge (from isomorphous substitution in the crystal lattice and broken bonds at edges). This attracts cations from the pore fluid, forming a diffuse double layer (DDL).

**Stern-Gouy model:** Inner compact layer (Stern layer) of firmly adsorbed cations, plus outer diffuse layer where cation concentration decreases exponentially with distance from the surface.

The thickness of the diffuse double layer controls clay behavior:

**Factors that decrease DDL thickness (flocculation tendency):**
- Increasing cation valence (Na⁺ → Ca²⁺ → Al³⁺)
- Increasing electrolyte concentration
- Decreasing dielectric constant
- Decreasing temperature (minor effect)

**Factors that increase DDL thickness (dispersion tendency):**
- Monovalent cations (Na⁺)
- Low electrolyte concentration
- High pH

## Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)

CEC measures the total quantity of exchangeable cations a soil can adsorb, expressed in milliequivalents per 100 grams (meq/100g). Governed by clay mineral type, amount of clay, organic matter content, and pH.

CEC is a key indicator of clay mineral type when direct mineralogical analysis is not available:
- CEC < 10 meq/100g → predominantly kaolinite
- CEC 10–40 meq/100g → illite dominant
- CEC > 40 meq/100g → smectite/montmorillonite present

## Swelling Behavior

Swelling potential depends on: clay mineral type (montmorillonite >> illite >> kaolinite), amount of clay fraction, pore fluid chemistry, initial moisture content, and confining stress.

**Free swell test:** Place dry soil in graduated cylinder with water — measure percent volume increase. Values > 100% indicate highly expansive soil.

**Swell pressure:** Maximum pressure developed when an expansive soil is prevented from swelling upon wetting. Measured by loading to prevent volume change during saturation (constant-volume method, D4546).

## Dispersive Clays

Dispersive clays deflocculate and erode in the presence of relatively pure water (low TDS). This is a chemical property, not related to particle size or Atterberg limits alone.

**Identification methods:**
- Pinhole test (D4647): Direct measure of erosion susceptibility under flowing water
- Double hydrometer test (D4221): Ratio of % fines from hydrometer without dispersant to % fines with dispersant. Ratio > 50% indicates dispersive.
- Crumb test: Place dry soil crumb in distilled water — observe if colloidal cloud forms
- Chemical analysis: Percent sodium (Na⁺ / total cations) > 40% suggests dispersive
- Sherard's classification chart: SAR vs. total dissolved salts in pore water

**Engineering Significance:** Dispersive clays are extremely dangerous in dam cores and canal embankments — they can self-pipe through internal erosion. Treatment: lime, gypsum, or cement stabilization; filters designed for the dispersed particle size.

## Sensitivity and Thixotropy

**Sensitivity (S_t):** Ratio of undisturbed undrained shear strength to remolded undrained shear strength at the same moisture content.

S_t = s_u(undisturbed) / s_u(remolded)

- S_t = 1–2: Insensitive
- S_t = 2–4: Low sensitivity  
- S_t = 4–8: Sensitive
- S_t = 8–16: Extra-sensitive
- S_t > 16: Quick clay

Quick clays (S_t > 16, often >> 30) occur in marine clays that have been leached by fresh water, removing the salt that stabilized the flocculated structure. Common in Scandinavia, eastern Canada, and some coastal deposits. Catastrophic flowslides can occur when disturbed.

**Thixotropy:** Time-dependent strength gain of remolded clay at constant moisture content. The remolded soil slowly regains some strength as particles rearrange. Significant for soft clays and drilled shaft construction.
