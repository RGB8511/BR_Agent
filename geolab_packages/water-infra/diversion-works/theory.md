# Diversion Works & Construction Hydraulics

## Purpose and Concept

River diversion is required during dam construction to keep the work area dry. Diversion design is a critical construction-phase engineering challenge — failure to divert floods during construction has caused major dam failures and construction disasters.

### Design Philosophy
The diversion system must safely pass floods that may occur during the construction period while maintaining the construction schedule. The acceptable risk level depends on consequences of overtopping the cofferdam and the stage of construction.

## Diversion Methods

### Tunnel Diversion
- Excavate one or more tunnels through the abutment rock
- Construct upstream and downstream cofferdams to dewater the river channel
- All flow passes through tunnel(s) during construction
- After dam completion, tunnels are plugged with concrete or converted to outlet works
- Most common for narrow valley sites in competent rock

### Channel Diversion
- Excavate a temporary or permanent channel around the dam site
- Cofferdams direct flow into the diversion channel
- Suitable for wide valley sites where rock tunneling is impractical
- Channel may be converted to spillway approach channel in final configuration

### Staged (Phased) Construction
- Build dam in stages, diverting flow through the partially completed structure
- Stage 1: Build one half of dam, route flow through other half
- Stage 2: Divert flow through completed portion, build second half
- Common for concrete dams on wide rivers (navigation dams, run-of-river)
- Requires notches or openings in the partially completed dam

### Culvert / Conduit Diversion
- Route flow through large culverts or conduits placed on the riverbed
- Cofferdams force flow into conduits
- Conduits later abandoned (encased in dam) or converted to permanent outlet works
- Suitable for smaller rivers and embankment dams

### Overtopping Design
For some embankment dam cofferdams, controlled overtopping may be accepted if:
- Cofferdam is designed to survive overtopping (armored crest and downstream face)
- Damage to ongoing construction is tolerable
- Risk of loss to life is negligible
- RCC cofferdams inherently resistant to overtopping

## Design Flood Selection

### Return Period
The diversion design flood is selected based on:
- Duration of exposure (construction period at risk)
- Consequences of overtopping (damage to construction, loss of life)
- Economic optimization (cost of diversion vs. cost of flood damage)

**Typical criteria:**
- USBR: 25-year flood for most cofferdams; higher for long exposure or high consequences
- USACE: 25-year or greater, based on risk analysis
- First filling: may require higher standard as incomplete dam is most vulnerable

### Seasonal Considerations
- Schedule cofferdam construction for low-flow season
- Design for floods during the expected construction window
- Reduce required diversion capacity by timing construction to avoid flood season

## Cofferdam Design

### Types
- **Earth/rockfill cofferdam:** Most common. Designed as a temporary embankment dam with impervious core or face. Must include seepage control.
- **Sheet pile cofferdam:** Steel sheet piles driven into foundation. Suitable for shallow foundations, moderate heads. Internal bracing required for deep cofferdams.
- **Concrete cofferdam:** Used when cofferdam becomes part of permanent structure.
- **Cellular cofferdam:** Interlocking steel sheet pile cells filled with granular material. Gravity structure. Used for deep water, high loads.
- **RCC cofferdam:** Can be designed to be overtopped. Often incorporated into permanent dam.

### Design Requirements
- Crest elevation: diversion flood level + freeboard (typically 0.5–1.5 m)
- Seepage control: adequate for the differential head
- Stability: sliding, overturning, bearing capacity (similar to dam design but lower safety factors acceptable for temporary structures)
- Foundation cutoff: sheet pile, grout curtain, or slurry wall to control underseepage

## River Closure

The most critical moment in construction — closing the gap to force flow into the diversion system.

### End-Dumping Method
- Progressively dump rock from both sides of the river into the closure gap
- Flow velocity through the narrowing gap increases — requires progressively larger rock
- Critical velocity through the gap determines the rock size needed for closure
- Complete closure during low-flow period

### Closure Hydraulics
As the gap narrows, velocity through the gap increases. At some point, the gap becomes an orifice or weir, and the upstream water level rises, creating flow through the diversion system.

**Stone stability in closure gap:**
d₅₀ = V² / (2g × Δ × C)

where V = gap velocity, Δ = (ρ_s/ρ_w - 1), C = stability coefficient (≈ 0.7–1.0).
